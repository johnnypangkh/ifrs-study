Chapter 2 — Recognition

Apply the recognition principle to initial and subsequent costs (spare parts, safety/env, replacements, major inspections) — capitalise vs expense. Official home for subsequent expenditure is IAS 16 · para 10–14.

Concept Map

2.0 Recognition principletop

The cost of an item of PPE is recognised as an asset if, and only if, it is probable that future economic benefits associated with the item will flow to the entity and the cost of the item can be measured reliably. IAS 16 · para 7 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.11 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.20.10

  • Two-limb test (Official). Recognise if, and only if:
    • (a) it is probable that future economic benefits associated with the item will flow to the entity; and
    • (b) the cost of the item can be measured reliably.
    IAS 16 · para 7 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.1 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.11
  • Risks and rewards timing (PwC). Sufficient certainty that future economic benefits will flow is normally achieved only when the risks and rewards of the asset have passed to the entity. In practice that often occurs on delivery. Contractual commitments for acquisition of PPE are disclosed, but the asset is not recognised until transfer of risks and rewards has occurred. PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.12 IAS 16 · para 74(c)
  • Same principle for every cost when incurred. The entity evaluates under this recognition principle all its PPE costs at the time they are incurred — costs incurred initially to acquire or construct an item, and costs incurred subsequently to add to, replace part of, or service it. IAS 16 · para 10 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.3 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.1 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.290.10
  • ROU depreciation may form part of PPE cost. The cost of an item of PPE may include costs relating to leases of assets used to construct, add to, replace part of or service PPE — such as depreciation of right-of-use assets. Detail of cost elements sits in Chapter 3 Initial measurement. IAS 16 · para 10 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.3

2.1 Unit of measure / aggregation of insignificant itemstop

IAS 16 does not prescribe the unit of measure for recognition — judgement is required. It may be appropriate to aggregate individually insignificant items (moulds, tools and dies) and apply the recognition criteria to the aggregate value. IAS 16 · para 9 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.13 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.4 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.10 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.10.25

  • Official silence on “what is an item”. Judgement covers how individual items are identified and the extent to which items are aggregated — including whether large items should be recognised as separate components. IAS 16 · para 9 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.13
  • Separately identifiable and individually significant (KPMG). An item that is separately identifiable and individually significant will generally constitute a unit of account under IAS 16. Assess significance considering all facts and circumstances (for example comparing the total cost of aggregated items to the cost of the item under review). Items of a dissimilar nature used in operations constitute separate classes and may not be aggregated. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.10.25 IAS 16 · para 9 IAS 16 · para 37
  • Componentisation still required for significant parts. Each part of an item of PPE with a cost that is significant in relation to the total cost of the item is depreciated separately (IAS 16 · para 43). Aggregation for recognition of insignificant items does not override that depreciation rule — see 2.8 and Chapter 5 Depreciation. DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.4 IAS 16 · para 43 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.14
  • Minor items / practical write-off (EY). Some businesses hold very large numbers of minor PPE items (spare parts, tools, pallets, returnable containers) used over more than one period. Practical control problems may lead entities to write off immaterial assets as expenses in the period of addition, or to immediately depreciate minor equipment to the same effect. Materiality judgements also often produce a minimum capitalisation threshold. EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.1 IAS 16 · para 9
  • Aggregation of properties — caution (EY). Official suggests aggregating moulds, tools and dies; it is silent on aggregating properties or similar assets that are individually immaterial purely by size. Aggregation that is inconsistent with broader IAS 16 requirements (for example revaluation by class, or monitoring assets individually) needs careful judgement. EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.10 IAS 16 · para 9

2.2 Spare parts, stand-by and servicing equipmenttop

Spare parts, stand-by equipment and servicing equipment are recognised as PPE when they meet the PPE definition; otherwise they are classified as inventory. IAS 16 · para 8 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.15 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.2 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.1 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.10.20

  • Definition test, not “used with PPE” test. Such items are PPE if they meet the definition, including the requirement to be used over more than one period. There is no requirement that they be used only in connection with an item of PPE. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.10.20 IAS 16 · para 8 IAS 16 · para BC12A
  • Major vs minor (PwC / EY). Spare parts and servicing equipment are normally treated as inventory and expensed as consumed. Major spare parts and stand-by equipment are treated as PPE where they meet the definition. Smaller spares may remain inventory; many entities set a practical minimum capitalisation value. PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.15 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.1 IAS 16 · para 8
  • Irregular use still qualifies. Expected use during more than one period does not require that use to be regular — a stand-by turbine held for continuity of production still meets the definition (see Illustration 2.2.1). DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.2-1 IAS 16 · para 8 IAS 16 · para 6
  • Core stock that operates another asset (EY). A minimum inventory level maintained on a continuing basis does not by itself turn inventory into PPE — each individual item may still be consumed within a single operating cycle. But where an item is necessary to operate or benefit from another asset over more than one operating cycle, and cannot be recouped through sale (or is significantly impaired after use), it is accounted for as PPE even if it cannot be physically separated from the rest of inventories (see Illustration 2.2.2). EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.5 IAS 16 · para 8 IAS 2 · para 6 IAS 16 · para 16(b)
See also — IAS 2. Spare parts that fail the PPE definition are classified as inventory under IAS 2. Core-stock vs PPE classification for cushion gas and similar items: Illustration 2.2.2. IAS 16 · para 8 IAS 2 · para 6 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.5

Illustration: 2.2.1 — Classification of stand-by equipmentDTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.2-1 IAS 16 · para 8 IAS 16 · para 6

Facts

  • An entity has installed two turbines.
  • One produces energy for the plant; the other is a back-up if the first turbine fails or is otherwise out of service.
  • The probability that the spare turbine will ever be used is very low.
  • The spare turbine is necessary to ensure continuity of the production process if the first turbine fails.
  • Useful life of the stand-by turbine equals the life of the plant (same as the primary turbine).

Assessment

  • IAS 16 · para 8: stand-by equipment qualifies as PPE when it meets the definition of PPE.
  • IAS 16 · para 6 requires expected use during more than one period — it does not require that use to be regular.
  • Low probability of activation does not defeat the definition when the item is held for use over the plant’s life.

Conclusion

  • Classify the spare turbine as PPE.
  • Depreciate from the date it becomes available for use (location and condition necessary to be capable of operating in the manner intended by management).
  • Depreciation of idle / stand-by equipment detail → Chapter 5 Depreciation.

Does irregular (or low-probability) use prevent stand-by equipment from being PPE?

Illustration: 2.2.2 — Core stock necessary to operate another asset (inventory vs PPE)EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.5 IAS 16 · para 8 IAS 2 · para 6 IAS 16 · para 16(b)

Facts (three cases)

  • Cave cushion gas. Right to use an underground cave for gas storage for 50 years. Cave filled with gas; a substantial part (25%) will only keep the cave under pressure so gas can be extracted. Molecules cannot be distinguished; the non-recoupable portion can still be measured.
  • Refinery heel. Oil refining plant must contain a minimum quantity of oil to operate. Oil can be taken out only when the plant is abandoned, then polluted so value is significantly reduced (saleable only as sludge).
  • Pipeline gas. Entity sells gas and always holds a quantity of gas in its distribution network.

Test (EY view)

  • Account for inventory as PPE if it: is not held for sale / consumed in production or services; is necessary to operate or benefit from another asset during more than one operating cycle; and either cannot be recouped through sale or is significantly impaired after that use.
  • Applies even if the PPE portion cannot be physically separated from remaining inventories.
  • Minimum inventory maintained on a continuing basis does not by itself turn inventory into PPE.

Conclusion

  • Cave: split volume — gas held for sale → IAS 2; gas held to keep pressure → PPE, depreciated over expected cave use.
  • Refinery heel: minimum crude necessary to operate and not recoupable (or significantly impaired) → PPE, amortised over plant life.
  • Pipeline gas: not necessary to operate the pipeline; held for sale/consumption → inventory.
  • Recognising non-recoupable operating stock as inventory would imply an immediate loss and would not reflect that the stock operates another asset over more than one cycle.

2.3 Initial costs vs subsequent — same recognition principletop

IAS 16 makes no distinction in principle between initial costs of acquiring or constructing an item and subsequent expenditure to add to, replace part of, or service it — every cost is tested under paragraph 7 when incurred. IAS 16 · para 10 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.3 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.1 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.61–22.62 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.290.10

  • Capitalise only if criteria met. If the recognition criteria are met, subsequent expenditure is added to the carrying amount of the PPE (or recognised as a separate asset, as appropriate). If not met, expense when incurred. DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.1 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.61 IAS 16 · para 7
  • Additional future benefits (PwC). Subsequent costs that merely maintain the economic benefits originally expected are repairs and maintenance → expense. Capitalise when it is probable that additional future economic benefits associated with the item will flow to the entity and cost can be measured reliably — typically increased productive capacity, additional ability to generate benefits, or extension of expected useful life. PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.62 IAS 16 · para 7
  • No “repairs vs improvements” labels (DTT / BC). Official does not distinguish subsequent expenditure that maintains existing service potential from expenditure that enhances it. All major subsequent expenditure is capitalised if probable future benefits will flow; any replaced part is derecognised. Day-to-day servicing is not capitalised even though arguably incurred in pursuit of future benefits, because it is not sufficiently certain under the general recognition principle. DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.5-1 IAS 16 · para BC5–BC12 IAS 16 · para 12
  • Relocating / reorganising (KPMG). Recognition of costs in the carrying amount ceases when the item is in the location and condition necessary for it to be capable of operating in the manner intended by management. Costs of relocating or reorganising part or all of an entity’s operations are not included in the carrying amount of an item of PPE. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.290.30 IAS 16 · para 20
  • Subsequent expenditure on an impaired asset (DTT). Capital expenditure on an item for which an impairment loss was previously recognised is still tested under IAS 16 · para 7. An entity will generally only incur additional expenditure on an impaired asset when it expects that expenditure to generate net future economic benefits — in which case the criteria are likely met. Same analysis for continuing expenditure on an asset under construction. DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.6-1 IAS 16 · para 7
  • Elements of cost / when capitalisation ceases → Chapter 3 Initial measurement (IAS 16 · para 15–28 · 20A). This chapter owns the capitalise-vs-expense recognition decision. IAS 16 · para 15 IAS 16 · para 20

Illustration: 2.3.1 — Remodelling a supermarket (subsequent expenditure)PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.62.2 IAS 16 · para 7 IAS 16 · para 10

Facts

  • Entity A, a supermarket chain, renovates one of its major stores.
  • After renovation the store will have more space for in-store promotion outlets and will include a restaurant.
  • Budgets for the year after re-opening include remodelling cost and an expected 15% increase in sales from attracting new customers.

Assessment

  • Remodelling creates additional future economic benefits (expected sales uplift).
  • Cost of remodelling can be measured reliably.
  • Same recognition principle applies to subsequent costs as to initial costs (IAS 16 · para 10).

Conclusion

  • Capitalise the remodelling expenditure.
  • Contrast: expenditure that merely maintains originally expected benefits → expense as repairs and maintenance (see 2.5).

Illustration: 2.3.2 — Clear capitalise vs clear expense (hotel wing and cleaning)PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.62.3 IAS 16 · para 7 IAS 16 · para 12

Facts

  • Capitalise end: cost of adding a new wing to a hotel — additional rooms increase revenue-earning capacity.
  • Expense end: cost of cleaning the hotel — period cost of servicing the hotel.
  • Some expenditure falls between these extremes and requires judgement.

Assessment

  • New wing: probable that future economic benefits will arise; cost reliably measurable → meets para 7.
  • Cleaning: day-to-day servicing that maintains originally expected benefits → expense under para 12.
  • Middle cases: apply the same two-limb test; analyse nature of costs (see Illustration 2.5.1).

  • New wing → capitalise.
  • Cleaning → expense as incurred.
  • Judgement zone: no shortcut label of “repair” vs “improvement” — apply para 7.

2.4 Safety and environmental assetstop

PPE acquired for safety or environmental reasons may qualify for recognition even if it does not directly increase the future economic benefits of any particular existing item — because it enables the entity to derive future economic benefits from related assets in excess of what could be derived without it. The combined carrying amount of such an asset and related assets is reviewed for impairment under IAS 36. IAS 16 · para 11 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.12 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.62.1 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.3 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.2 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.290.40

  • Official chemical-plant example and subsequent filters required by law. A chemical manufacturer may install new chemical-handling processes to comply with environmental requirements; without them the entity cannot manufacture and sell chemicals. The same paragraph 11 test covers a new grade of emissions filter that aluminium smelters must fit, or they cannot operate: capitalise the filters as PPE so long as the total carrying amount of the smelter plus the filters does not exceed recoverable amount. Both fact patterns are in Illustration 2.4.1. IAS 16 · para 11 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · Example 3.3 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · Example 16 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.290.40 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.62.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.2
  • Voluntary environmental investment (EY view). An entity may voluntarily invest in environmental equipment without a legal requirement. Capitalise if the expenditure meets the definition of an asset (for example increases economic life of the related asset or enables additional benefits from other assets), or if a constructive obligation exists to invest and the expenditure directly improves environmental/safety standards and is not repairs/maintenance or period/operating cost. EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.2 IAS 16 · para 11
  • Combined carrying amount → IAS 36. Whenever safety and environmental assets are capitalised, review the resulting carrying amount of that asset and related assets for impairment so the combined carrying amount does not exceed combined recoverable amount. IAS 16 · para 11 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.12 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.2
See also — IAS 36. Safety/environmental capitalisation triggers a combined-carrying-amount impairment review under IAS 36. This pack does not teach IAS 36 as a chapter. IAS 16 · para 11 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.12 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.2

Illustration: 2.4.1 — Environmental plant enhancements and legally required emissions filtersIAS 16 · para 11 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · Example 3.3 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.62.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.1.2 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · Example 16 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.290.40

Facts

  • A chemical manufacturer must install new chemical-handling processes to comply with environmental requirements for production and storage of dangerous chemicals.
  • The enhancements do not by themselves generate economic benefits independently of the related plant.
  • Without the enhancements the entity cannot manufacture and sell chemicals (plant may have to close).
  • Separately, a government introduces a new emissions law requiring aluminium smelters to be fitted with a new grade of filter.
  • Unless the new filters are fitted, the smelters are no longer permitted to operate.
  • The filters themselves do not give rise directly to future economic benefits. They are a condition of continuing to operate the smelter.

Assessment

  • Acquisition for safety or environmental reasons may not directly increase the benefits of any particular existing item.
  • The items qualify because they enable future economic benefits from related assets in excess of what could be derived without them.
  • Safety or environmental equipment may be recognised as a separate item of PPE on that basis.
  • The chemical-handling processes and the emissions filters are the same paragraph 11 test. Neither fact pattern adds a further recognition limb.
  • After capitalisation, review the combined carrying amount of the safety or environmental asset and the related plant under IAS 36.

Conclusion

  • Recognise the chemical-plant enhancements as PPE, only to the extent recoverable when grouped with the related plant.
  • Capitalise the new filters as PPE as long as the total carrying amount of the smelter plus the new filters does not exceed recoverable amount.
  • The recoverable-amount limb is the same IAS 36 review for both patterns.
  • The impairment review is part of the recognition package.

2.5 Day-to-day servicing → profit or losstop

Under the recognition principle in paragraph 7, an entity does not recognise in the carrying amount of an item of PPE the costs of day-to-day servicing of the item. Those costs are recognised in profit or loss as incurred. IAS 16 · para 12 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.63 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.2 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.3 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.290.20

  • What day-to-day servicing comprises. Primarily costs of labour and consumables, and may include the cost of small parts. The purpose is often described as ‘repairs and maintenance’. IAS 16 · para 12 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.63
  • Why they fail capitalisation. These costs maintain the asset’s potential to deliver the future economic benefits expected when the asset was originally acquired — they do not add to those benefits. PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.63 IAS 16 · para 12
  • Analyse mixed invoices. When an entity incurs both maintenance costs and substantial modification costs on the same item, analyse expenditure by nature: day-to-day servicing → P&L; substantial modification → capitalise if para 7 met; derecognise any replaced part (see Illustration 2.5.1). DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.5-1 IAS 16 · para 12 IAS 16 · para 7

Illustration: 2.5.1 — Annual redecoration vs new partitioningDTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.5-1 IAS 16 · para 12 IAS 16 · para 7 IAS 16 · para BC5–BC12

Facts

  • A retail outlet is redecorated each year. Expenditure is regular and not particularly large — treated as day-to-day servicing.
  • In the current year the entity asks the supplier also to install new partitioning intended to make the outlet more profitable and result in additional future economic benefits.
  • New partitioning may replace existing partitioning.

Assessment

  • Official does not use “repairs and maintenance” vs “improvements” labels as the decision rule — apply para 7 to major subsequent expenditure; day-to-day servicing stays out under para 12 / BC.
  • Analyse the invoice by nature: redecoration vs partitioning.
  • Partitioning: measure cost reliably? Probable additional future benefits? If yes → capitalise. If it replaces existing partitioning → derecognise the replaced part.

Conclusion

  • Redecoration costs → expense in P&L (IAS 16 · para 12).
  • Incremental partitioning costs → capitalise if para 7 criteria met.
  • To the extent new partitioning replaces existing partitioning → derecognise the replaced partitioning.

2.6 Replacements — capitalise and derecognise replaced parttop

Under the recognition principle in paragraph 7, an entity recognises in the carrying amount of an item of PPE the cost of replacing part of such an item when that cost is incurred if the recognition criteria are met. The carrying amount of those parts that are replaced is derecognised in accordance with the derecognition provisions (IAS 16 · para 67–72). IAS 16 · para 13 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.3 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.3.1 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.86

  • Official examples of parts. Parts of some items may require replacement at regular intervals — for example a furnace may require relining after a specified number of hours of use, or aircraft interiors such as seats and galleys may require replacement several times during the life of the airframe. Items may also be acquired for less frequently recurring replacement (interior walls of a building) or nonrecurring replacement. IAS 16 · para 13 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.3.1
  • Not automatic capitalisation. Official does not state that replacement expenditures necessarily qualify for recognition — capitalise when incurred if the recognition criteria are met. Aircraft interiors are clearly best treated as separate assets with useful lives different from the airframe; interior walls may extend useful life, upgrade capacity, improve quality, or reduce operating costs. EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.3.1 IAS 16 · para 13
  • Derecognise even if not separately depreciated. If the cost of the replacement is recognised in the carrying amount, derecognise the carrying amount of what was replaced — regardless of whether it had been identified as a component and depreciated separately — so that replacement and replaced item are not both carried as assets (see Illustration 2.6.1). DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.3 IAS 16 · para 13 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.3.1
  • Estimating the replaced carrying amount (para 70). If it is not practicable to determine the carrying amount of the replaced part, the entity may use the cost of the replacement as an indication of what the cost of the replaced part was at the time it was acquired or constructed — often applied on a depreciated replacement-cost basis (see Illustration 2.6.2). An entity may not actually identify the parts until it incurs the replacement expenditure. IAS 16 · para 70 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.3.1 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.3 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.86
  • Replacement roof meeting recognition criteria (PwC). A building acquired that requires substantial renovation such as a new roof: subsequent cost of replacing the roof is capitalised when it meets the asset recognition criteria — it increases future economic benefits expected from the building and can be reliably measured. Components are not separate classes and are not separately disclosed. Component depreciation of the roof vs remainder → Chapter 5. PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.14.1 IAS 16 · para 13 IAS 16 · para 43
See also — Chapter 6 Derecognition. Mechanics of removing the replaced part’s carrying amount, and gain/loss on derecognition, live in Chapter 6 (IAS 16 · para 67–72 · 70). This chapter owns the capitalise-and-derecognise decision at replacement. IAS 16 · para 13 IAS 16 · para 67–72

Illustration: 2.6.1 — Hotel refurbishment — capitalise replacement and derecognise prior assetsDTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.3-1 IAS 16 · para 13 IAS 16 · para 7

Facts

  • A hotel operator refurbishes its hotels every ten years, on average.
  • The cost of refurbishment is considered a replacement of assets capitalised (recognition criteria in IAS 16 · para 7 are met).

Assessment

  • Capitalise the refurbishment cost in the carrying amount when incurred if para 7 is met.
  • The replacement indicates that previously recognised assets may now need to be derecognised.
  • Derecognition typically gives rise to a loss to the extent those prior assets have not already been depreciated.

Conclusion

  • Capitalise refurbishment as replacement.
  • Derecognise carrying amounts of replaced previously recognised assets.
  • Gain/loss presentation → Chapter 6 Derecognition.

2.7 Major inspectionstop

A condition of continuing to operate an item of PPE (for example an aircraft) may be performing regular major inspections for faults regardless of whether parts of the item are replaced. When each major inspection is performed, its cost is recognised in the carrying amount of the item as a replacement if the recognition criteria are satisfied. Any remaining carrying amount of the cost of the previous inspection (as distinct from physical parts) is derecognised. IAS 16 · para 14 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.64 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.4 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.3.2 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.250.10

  • Inspection component distinct from physical parts. Derecognise any remaining previous-inspection carrying amount regardless of whether the cost of the previous inspection was identified in the transaction in which the item was acquired or constructed. If necessary, the estimated cost of a future similar inspection may be used as an indication of what the cost of the existing inspection component was when the item was acquired or constructed. IAS 16 · para 14 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.64 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.4
  • Not an IAS 37 provision. IAS 37 prohibits providing in advance for an overhaul of owned plant with no present obligation. Instead add the major-inspection cost to the asset when performed (if para 7 met) and depreciate over the period until the next major inspection. EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 3.3.2 IAS 37 · IE Example 11A–11B
  • Identify at acquisition when significant (PwC / KPMG). If inspection/overhaul represents a significant part of the asset’s cost, a proportion equivalent to expected overhaul cost is often identified and depreciated over the period to the next inspection; when the actual overhaul occurs, capitalise it (if criteria met), derecognise the remaining prior inspection CA, and depreciate the new inspection cost to the next event. Remainder of the asset is depreciated over its full useful life on the basis that appropriate overhauls will be carried out when due (see Illustration 2.7.1). PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.64.1 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.250 IAS 16 · para 14
  • Current market price for embedded component (KPMG). If a major inspection or overhaul cost is embedded in the acquisition cost, estimate the carrying amount of the component by reference to the current market price of such overhauls, not the expected future price. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.250.20 IAS 16 · para 13–14
  • Routine repairs stay out. Component accounting for inspection/overhaul is for major expenditure at regular intervals; costs associated with routine repairs and maintenance are expensed as incurred. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.250.40 IAS 16 · para 12

Illustration: 2.7.1 — Major inspection / overhaul as a replacement componentIAS 16 · para 14 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.64.1 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · Example 15A DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 3.5.4

Facts

  • Aircraft (PwC). Required by law to be inspected/overhauled every three years. Overhaul is a significant part of the asset’s cost.
  • Ship (KPMG Example 15A). New ship acquired for 400; useful life 15 years; dry-docked every three years for major overhaul. At acquisition, dry-docking costs for similar three-year-old ships ≈ 80.

Assessment

  • Identify a proportion of cost equivalent to expected overhaul / use current market dry-docking price (80) as the inspection component.
  • Depreciate that component over the period to the next inspection/overhaul (three years).
  • When the actual inspection/overhaul is performed: capitalise actual cost if para 7 met; derecognise any remaining previous-inspection CA; depreciate new inspection cost to the next event.
  • Remainder of the asset (ship body etc.) depreciated over its own useful life on the basis that overhauls will be carried out when due.

Conclusion

  • Ship: dry-docking component cost = 80 (depreciate over 3 years); remaining CA 320 (may need further component splits) — KPMG Example 15A.
  • Aircraft: capitalise each mandatory inspection/overhaul; derecognise prior inspection CA to avoid double counting — PwC FAQ 22.64.1.
  • If previous inspection was not separately identified, estimate using cost of a future similar inspection as proxy (IAS 16 · para 14).
  • Further component-cost allocation / early dry-dock write-off detail → Chapter 5 Depreciation (KPMG 3.2.250–3.2.280).
