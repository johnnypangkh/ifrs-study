Chapter 5 — Depreciation

Allocate depreciable amount systematically over useful life; depreciate significant parts separately; review residual value, useful life, and method at least annually.

Concept Map

5.0 What depreciation istop

Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of an asset over its useful life. Depreciable amount is the cost of an asset, or other amount substituted for cost, less its residual value. Useful life is the period over which an asset is expected to be available for use by an entity, or the number of production or similar units expected to be obtained from the asset by an entity. IAS 16 · para 6 IAS 16 · para 50 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.82 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.1 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.140.10

  • Allocation, not valuation. The purpose is to allocate cost (or revalued amount) systematically over useful life — not to recognise decreases in market value. Depreciation is recognised even if fair value exceeds carrying amount, as long as residual value does not exceed carrying amount. Repair and maintenance do not negate the need to depreciate. IAS 16 · para 52 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.140.40 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.1
  • Applies under both measurement models. Cost model and revaluation model both require depreciation of depreciable amount — models live in Chapter 4; this chapter is the allocation engine. IAS 16 · para 30 IAS 16 · para 31 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.80
  • Identify five inputs for each separately depreciable component (DTT). Significant parts; cost or valuation of each; residual value; useful life; method that reflects consumption. DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.1
  • Unlimited useful life. An asset with an unlimited useful life (typically freehold land not subject to depletion) is not depreciated. IAS 16 · para 58 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.80
See also — Chapter 4 Subsequent measurement — models. Choosing cost vs revaluation for a class is already taught there — this chapter does not re-dump those peers. IAS 16 · para 29–42

Illustration: 5.0.1 — Rolling hotel-equipment replacements do not replace depreciationPwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.83.2 IAS 16 · para 52

Facts

  • An entity operates several hotels.
  • Total carrying amount of hotel equipment (bed linen, dishes and similar) remains consistent year to year.
  • All equipment is replaced every few years under a rolling programme so replacement spend is roughly level each year.
  • Management proposes to stop depreciating the equipment and expense replacements each year instead.

Assessment

  • Assets with useful lives of more than one year are capitalised and depreciated over those lives.
  • Similar items may be grouped as a single asset for efficiency, with depreciation calculated for the group.
  • Expensing all replacements while omitting depreciation on capitalised assets is not an acceptable substitute for systematic allocation.
  • Repair and maintenance (or rolling replacement spend) do not negate the need to depreciate (para 52).

Conclusion

  • Continue to capitalise equipment with multi-period useful lives and depreciate systematically.
  • Reject the policy of expensing replacements instead of depreciating.

5.1 Depreciable amount — cost (or substituted amount) minus residual valuetop

The depreciable amount of an asset shall be allocated on a systematic basis over its useful life. Depreciable amount is determined after deducting residual value. Residual value is the estimated amount that an entity would currently obtain from disposal of the asset, after deducting the estimated costs of disposal, if the asset were already of the age and in the condition expected at the end of its useful life. Past events that change residual value affect depreciable amount; expected future residual changes do not. Ships, aircraft and similar assets may have significant, volatile residuals. IAS 16 · para 53 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.150.20 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.3

Illustration: 5.1.1 — Residual value: second-hand sale versus scrapKPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · Example 10A KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · Example 10B IAS 16 · para 6 IAS 16 · para 53

Facts

  • Company M (10A). Buys a machine costing 400. Plans to use it for three years then sell on the second-hand market. At acquisition, a three-year-old machine trades for 150.
  • Company N (10B). Buys a similar machine costing 400. Plans to hold until the end of its five-year economic life then sell as scrap. At acquisition, scrap-steel quote is 50.

Assessment

  • Residual value is the amount currently obtainable for an asset already of end-of-useful-life age and condition.
  • M residual at acquisition = 150 (current second-hand price for a three-year-old machine).
  • N residual at acquisition = 50 (current scrap quote).
  • Different intended holding policies produce different residuals and different depreciable amounts for otherwise similar machines.

Conclusion

  • M: depreciable amount over three years = 250 (400 − 150).
  • N: depreciable amount over five years = 350 (400 − 50).
  • Match residual to the entity’s intended end-of-use disposal path, using current market evidence.

Illustration: 5.1.2 — Change in residual value estimate — ship scrap steelKPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · Example 11 IAS 16 · para 51 IAS 16 · para 54

Facts

  • On 1 January 2019, Company S buys a ship costing 4,000.
  • Plans to hold until end of 25-year economic life then sell as scrap.
  • At acquisition, scrap-steel broker quote = 500 → depreciable amount 3,500; 2019 depreciation 140 (3,500 / 25).
  • December 2019: steel prices fall; new quote → residual at 31 December 2019 = 260.

Assessment

  • Residual value is reviewed at least annually; change = prospective estimate change (IAS 8).
  • Carrying amount after 2019 depreciation = 4,000 − 140 = 3,860.
  • New depreciable amount for remaining life = 3,860 − 260 = 3,600.
  • Remaining useful life = 24 years.

Conclusion

  • Annual depreciation from 2020 = 150 (3,600 / 24).
  • Do not restate prior periods; do not pull future inflation into residual.

5.2 Significant parts — component accountingtop

Each part of an item of property, plant and equipment with a cost that is significant in relation to the total cost of the item shall be depreciated separately. An entity allocates the amount initially recognised to its significant parts and depreciates each such part separately. For example, it may be appropriate to depreciate separately the airframe and engines of an aircraft. Similarly, if an entity acquires PPE subject to an operating lease in which it is the lessor, it may be appropriate to depreciate separately amounts attributable to favourable or unfavourable lease terms relative to market terms. IAS 16 · para 43 IAS 16 · para 44 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.85 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.2 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.2 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.230.10

  • Group parts with the same useful life and method. Significant parts that share useful life and depreciation method may be grouped in determining the depreciation charge. IAS 16 · para 45
  • Depreciate the remainder separately. The remainder comprises parts that are individually not significant. If expectations for those parts vary, approximation techniques may be needed so the remainder faithfully represents consumption pattern and/or useful life. An entity may also choose to depreciate separately parts that are not cost-significant. IAS 16 · para 46 IAS 16 · para 47 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.230.30
  • Physical and non-physical components. A separate component may be a physical part (for example seating in a stadium) or a non-physical part representing a major inspection or overhaul. Component accounting is mandatory when applicable, but infinite splitting is not required when the effect would be immaterial. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.230.10 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.230.20 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.2-1
  • Disclosure still by class. Components are accounted for separately for depreciation, but financial statements generally continue to disclose a single class (for example aircraft, not body/hydraulics/engines separately). KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.230.50
  • Replacement / major inspection bridge. When a significant component is replaced, derecognise the replaced carrying amount and capitalise the new component if recognition criteria are met — recognition home in Chapter 2; depreciation of the inspection/overhaul component is taught here. IAS 16 · para 13 IAS 16 · para 14 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.86

Illustration: 5.2.1 — Component depreciation of a factory that requires periodic roof replacementPwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.14.2 IAS 16 · para 43

Facts

  • Small manufacturing company acquires a new factory for C1m; residual value C100,000.
  • Flat roof needs replacing every 10 years at a cost of C100,000; roof cost is significant relative to total cost.
  • Useful economic life of the factory = 30 years.
  • Approach A: treat as one asset; depreciate whole factory over 30 years → C30,000 p.a.
  • Approach B: roof C100,000 over 10 years → C10,000 p.a.; remainder C900,000 down to residual C100,000 over 30 years → C26,667 p.a.

Assessment

  • Significant components must be depreciated separately (para 43).
  • Approach A understates early-year consumption of the roof’s service potential and mis-matches the replacement cycle.
  • Approach B allocates roof cost over its 10-year replacement cycle and the building shell over 30 years to residual.
  • Combined annual charge under B = C36,667, even across the factory’s life.

Conclusion

  • Approach B is correct.
  • Do not depreciate a significant short-lived roof as if it had the building’s 30-year life.

5.3 Useful life — factors; land and buildingstop

Useful life is defined in terms of the asset’s expected utility to the entity. The future economic benefits embodied in an asset are consumed principally through use; other factors such as technical or commercial obsolescence and wear and tear while idle often reduce benefits that might otherwise have been obtained. Consequently all of the following factors are considered: IAS 16 · para 56 IAS 16 · para 57 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.98 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.4.4 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.4

  • (a) Expected usage — assessed by reference to expected capacity or physical output.
  • (b) Expected physical wear and tear — depends on operational factors (for example number of shifts), repair and maintenance programme, and care while idle.
  • (c) Technical or commercial obsolescence — from changes or improvements in production, or change in market demand for the product or service output. Expected future reductions in selling price of items produced using the asset may indicate obsolescence.
  • (d) Legal or similar limits — such as expiry dates of related leases.
  • Useful life may be shorter than economic life. Asset-management policy (for example dispose of vehicles after a set period) can shorten useful life relative to economic life. Estimation is a matter of judgement based on experience with similar assets. IAS 16 · para 57 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.4.4.2 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.4
  • Land and buildings are separable assets even when acquired together. Land with unlimited useful life is not depreciated. Buildings have limited useful life and are depreciable. An increase in the value of land on which a building stands does not affect the building’s depreciable amount or useful life. IAS 16 · para 58 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.87 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.4.2 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.6.1
  • Land with limited useful life. Quarries, mines, landfill and similar land may be depreciated in a manner that reflects benefits to be derived. If cost of land includes site dismantlement, removal and restoration, that portion is depreciated over the period of benefits obtained by incurring those costs. IAS 16 · para 58 IAS 16 · para 59 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.4.2
  • Historic buildings are not exempt. Very long useful life and/or high residual value may make the charge small, but depreciation is still required; identify shorter-lived components (for example roofs). DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.6.2-1 IAS 16 · para 58

Illustration: 5.3.1 — Landfill site — land and restoration obligation both depreciatedPwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.80.1 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.87.1 IAS 16 · para 58–59

Facts

  • A landfill site will need to be restored when it is no longer used.
  • Carrying amount of the land asset may include costs of site dismantling, removal and restoration.
  • The site itself has a limited useful life to the landfill operator; residual value may be considerably less than original cost.

Assessment

  • Restoration cost component is depreciated over the period during which economic benefits are obtained (while refuse is dumped).
  • Land with limited useful life is depreciated over that useful life to residual value.
  • Separate the unlimited-life land element (if any) from the restoration / limited-life land elements.

Conclusion

  • Depreciate both the restoration obligation capitalised into PPE and the limited-life land element over the landfill operating period.
  • Do not treat all land as non-depreciable when benefits are time-limited.

5.4 When depreciation begins and ceasestop

Depreciation of an asset begins when it is available for use — that is, when it is in the location and condition necessary for it to be capable of operating in the manner intended by management. Depreciation ceases at the earlier of the date that the asset is classified as held for sale (or included in a disposal group classified as held for sale) in accordance with IFRS 5, and the date that the asset is derecognised. Therefore depreciation does not cease when the asset becomes idle or is retired from active use unless the asset is fully depreciated. However, under usage methods the depreciation charge can be zero while there is no production. IAS 16 · para 55 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.90 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.4.2 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.4.3 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.5 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.220.10

  • Available for use equals the same point as ceasing to capitalise costs. Once the asset is capable of operating as intended, stop adding to cost and start depreciating. DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.4.2 IAS 16 · para 20
  • Idle does not mean cease. Temporary shutdown, mothballing, or delayed start of use after readiness does not stop depreciation (except under a pure usage method with zero output). Retirement from active use may be an IAS 36 impairment indicator. IAS 16 · para 55 IAS 36 · para 12
See also — IFRS 5 Non-current Assets Held for Sale and Discontinued Operations. Classification as held for sale (or in a disposal group held for sale) stops IAS 16 depreciation at that earlier date. IFRS 5 IAS 16 · para 55

Facts

  • Company Q uses specialised machinery; it is the only entity in its market operating that type.
  • Q will introduce new specialised machinery and discontinue use of the current machines.
  • To protect competitive advantage, Q will not sell the old machinery; it will mothball them for potential further use or later sale.

  • Neither held-for-sale classification under IFRS 5 nor derecognition has occurred.
  • Para 55: depreciation does not cease when the asset becomes idle or is retired from active use unless fully depreciated.

  • Retirement is likely an IAS 36 impairment indicator; here fair value less costs of disposal exceeds carrying amount so no impairment loss.

  • Continue to depreciate the mothballed machinery systematically over its estimated useful life to Q.
  • Do not stop depreciation merely because production has ceased.

5.5 Depreciation methodstop

The depreciation method used shall reflect the pattern in which the asset’s future economic benefits are expected to be consumed by the entity. The method applied shall be reviewed at least at each financial year-end and, if there has been a significant change in the expected pattern of consumption, the method shall be changed to reflect the changed pattern. Such a change is accounted for as a change in an accounting estimate in accordance with IAS 8. IAS 16 · para 60 IAS 16 · para 61 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.93 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.5.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.6 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.160.10

  • Straight-line — constant charge over useful life if residual value does not change. IAS 16 · para 62
  • Diminishing balance — decreasing charge over useful life. IAS 16 · para 62
  • Units of production — charge based on expected use or output. Common in extractive industries. IAS 16 · para 62 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.5.2 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.6.2
  • Select for consumption pattern, then apply consistently unless the expected pattern changes. Tax-allowance methods (for example double declining solely because of tax) are not permitted unless they also reflect consumption. Increasing-charge / sinking-fund / annuity methods that track return or time value of money rather than consumption are not appropriate; units of production may be justified when it reflects physical consumption. DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.5.2 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.5.3-1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.6

5.6 Revenue-based method restrictiontop

A depreciation method that is based on revenue that is generated by an activity that includes the use of an asset is not appropriate. The revenue generated by an activity that includes the use of an asset generally reflects factors other than the consumption of the economic benefits of the asset. For example, revenue is affected by other inputs and processes, selling activities and changes in sales volumes and prices. The price component of revenue may be affected by inflation, which has no bearing upon the way in which an asset is consumed. IAS 16 · para 62A PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.98 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.5.4 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.6 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.210

  • Hard restriction, not a soft preference. Revenue-based depreciation (for example ratio of period revenue to total expected revenue) does not comply with the para 60 consumption-pattern requirement. IAS 16 · BC33B IAS 16 · BC33E DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 7.5.4
  • Activity-based methods remain available. Units of production and similar methods that track physical output or usage can be appropriate; they are not the same as revenue-based methods. EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 5.6 IAS 16 · para 62

Not appropriate

  • Depreciation keyed to revenue generated by an activity that uses the asset.
  • Reasons: selling prices, volumes, other inputs/processes, inflation — none of these measure consumption of the asset’s economic benefits.

May be appropriate

  • Straight-line, diminishing balance, or units of production when the method reflects the expected consumption pattern.
  • Review method at least annually; change prospectively if the pattern changes.

5.7 Where the depreciation charge goestop

The depreciation charge for each period shall be recognised in profit or loss unless it is included in the carrying amount of another asset. Sometimes the future economic benefits embodied in an asset are absorbed in producing other assets — for example depreciation of manufacturing plant included in costs of conversion of inventories (IAS 2), or depreciation of PPE used for development included in the cost of an intangible asset (IAS 38). Illustration: 5.7.1 — Factory depreciation absorbed into self-constructed toolsPwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.84.2 IAS 16 · para 49

Facts

  • Entity A manufactures car components; customers continually introduce new models.
  • A develops new tools for its production processes; tool costs are capitalised and depreciated over three years.
  • One factory houses all production; 10% of the factory is used for development and construction of the tools.
  • Building is depreciated straight-line.

Assessment

  • Depreciation of PPE used to produce another asset is included in that asset’s cost when benefits are absorbed in production (para 49).
  • 10% of factory depreciation is a directly attributable cost of producing the tools.
  • That portion is capitalised into the tools and then depreciated with the tools over three years.

Conclusion

  • Include 10% of factory depreciation in the capitalised cost of the tools.
  • Related pattern: mining earth-moving equipment depreciation capitalised into mine cost (PwC FAQ 22.84.1).

5.8 Change in estimate — residual value, useful life, methodtop

Residual value and useful life shall be reviewed at least at each financial year-end and, if expectations differ from previous estimates, the change(s) shall be accounted for as a change in an accounting estimate in accordance with IAS 8. The depreciation method shall likewise be reviewed at least at each financial year-end; a significant change in the expected pattern of consumption changes the method prospectively as an estimate change, not as an IAS 8 policy change. Facts

  • KPMG Example 9: printing machine acquired beginning 2016; original useful life 10 years. End of 2018 carrying amount = 240. Beginning 2019 remaining life revised to a further two years.
  • DTT 7.4.5-1: plant cost CU1.2m; useful life 10 years. End Year 3 carrying amount CU840,000. During Year 4 remaining useful life revised to four years; not impaired.
  • PwC FAQ 22.100.1: asset cost C100,000; useful life 10 years; nil residual; straight-line C10,000 p.a. On 1 Jan 20X4 net book value C60,000; remaining life revised to four years (total life eight).

Assessment

  • All three sources apply the same rule: depreciate carrying amount at revision over the revised remaining life.
  • KPMG numeric path — 240 over two years.
  • DTT numeric path — 840,000 / 4 = CU210,000 p.a. for Years 4–7.
  • PwC numeric path — 60,000 / 4 = C15,000 p.a. for the next four years.
  • Also consider whether a shortened life is an IAS 36 impairment indicator.

Conclusion

  • Prospective allocation of remaining carrying amount; no prior-period restatement.
  • Same mechanics apply if method changes because the consumption pattern changed (see next Illustration).

Facts

  • Asset acquired three years ago at cost CU5 million; method 10% reducing balance.
  • End of Year 3 carrying amount CU3,645,000.
  • Entity estimates remaining useful life eight years and switches to straight-line to reflect the revised consumption pattern.

Assessment

  • Method change for a changed consumption pattern = estimate change (IAS 8), not policy change.
  • Write off carrying amount using the new method over remaining life from the period of change.
  • Years 1–3 already charged under reducing balance (500 / 450 / 405 CU‘000).
  • Years 4–11: approximately CU456k p.a. straight-line on 3,645k over eight years (final years rounded).

Conclusion

  • Apply straight-line prospectively to remaining carrying amount; disclose material effects.
  • Do not restate Years 1–3 under the new method.

Illustration: 5.8.3 — Revising residual value — including residual rising above carrying amountPwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.101.1 IAS 16 · para 51 IAS 16 · para 54

Facts

  • Asset bought for C1,000; useful life six years; residual at acquisition C70.
  • End of year 3 carrying amount C535; accumulated depreciation C465.
  • End of year 4: residual revised to C100 based on prices then (not future inflation to C400).
  • If in year 5 residual is revised to C400 (current prices), residual would exceed the remaining depreciable path.

Assessment

  • Year 4: depreciable amount becomes C900; less depreciation to date C465 → C435 over remaining three years → C145 in year 4.
  • Use current residual (prices at review date), not an inflated future residual.
  • If later residual meets or exceeds the path implied by accumulated depreciation / carrying amount, charge goes to nil; excess previously charged is not reinstated.

Conclusion

  • Year 4 depreciation C145; if residual later rises to C400, years 5–6 depreciation = nil.
  • Para 54 zero-charge rule in action when residual catches or exceeds carrying amount.

5.9 Right-of-use depreciation tiptop

IFRS 16 right-of-use assets often borrow IAS 16 depreciation requirements. Depreciation of a right-of-use asset begins at lease commencement. Useful life of non-removable leasehold improvements is determined under IAS 16.56–57; if the improvements are not expected to be used beyond the lease term, useful life equals the lease term (IFRS IC November 2019).
