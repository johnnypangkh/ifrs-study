Chapter 3 — Initial measurement

Build cost at recognition: purchase price + directly attributable costs + initial dismantling/restoration; measure cash-price / exchanges; stop capitalising when capable of operating as intended; apply proceeds-before-intended-use (20A).

3.1 Elements of cost — three limbstop

The cost of an item of PPE comprises three limbs: (a) purchase price; (b) costs directly attributable to bringing the asset to the location and condition necessary for it to be capable of operating in the manner intended by management; and (c) the initial estimate of dismantling, removal and restoration costs (when the obligation arises on acquisition or from use other than producing inventories).   • Purchase price (limb (a)). Includes import duties and non-refundable purchase taxes, after deducting trade discounts and rebates. IAS 16 · para 16(a)
  • Directly attributable costs (limb (b)). Bring the asset to the location and condition necessary for it to be capable of operating in the manner intended by management — examples in 3.2; exclusions and stop-capitalising rules in 3.3. IAS 16 · para 16(b)

Examples of directly attributable costs include employee benefits arising directly from construction or acquisition, site preparation, initial delivery and handling, installation and assembly, costs of testing whether the asset is functioning properly, and professional fees. Illustration: 3.2.1 — Training costs are not part of PPE costDTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.2.1.2-1 IAS 16 · para 19(c) IAS 16 · para 20 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.30.70

Facts

  • An entity acquires equipment of a type its employees have never operated before.
  • During the installation period, employees receive extensive training on the equipment.
  • Costs include incremental fees for experts who conduct the training, and wages of employees during the training period.
  • The entity’s view: the equipment could not be used unless employees received the training.
  • If a third party installs the asset and training is bundled in the contract price, part of the total price may need allocation to training (KPMG).

Assessment

  • IAS 16 · para 19(c): costs of conducting business in a new location or with a new class of customer — including costs of staff training — are not costs of an item of PPE.
  • Training does not bring the asset to the location and condition necessary for it to be capable of operating as intended — the asset would be capable of operating without the training even if employees would not know how to operate it.
  • IAS 38 typically requires training costs to be expensed when incurred (DTT cross-ref).

Conclusion

  • Expense training costs (expert fees and employee wages during training) when incurred.
  • Do not capitalise them into PPE cost.
  • Allocate and expense any training portion embedded in a third-party installation contract (KPMG).

Illustration: 3.2.2 — Existing engineer’s labour during installation is capitalisedKPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · Example 1A IAS 16 · para 16(b) IAS 16 · para 17(a)

Facts

  • Company G is installing a major piece of equipment at one of its factories.
  • One of G’s existing engineers is assigned to manage the installation on a full-time basis.
  • Installation is expected to take six weeks.
  • The engineer’s costs (including all employee benefits) would have been incurred even if the installation had not taken place.

Assessment

  • Directly attributable expenditure need not be external or incremental (KPMG 3.2.30.50).
  • Employee benefits arising directly from construction or acquisition are Official examples of directly attributable costs (IAS 16 · para 17(a)).
  • The engineer’s full-time assignment during installation links the cost to bringing the asset to working condition.

Conclusion

  • Capitalise the engineer’s cost — including all employee benefits — for the installation period into the cost of the equipment.
  • “Would have been incurred anyway” does not defeat capitalisation when the cost is directly attributable.

Illustration: 3.2.3 — Supermarket renovation: capitalise remodelling, expense utilitiesPwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.19.1 IAS 16 · para 16(b)

Facts

  • Entity A operates a major chain of supermarkets and has acquired a new store location.
  • Significant renovation is required; management expects renovations to last three months, during which the supermarket will be closed.
  • Budget includes: construction and remodelling costs; salaries of staff preparing the store before opening; related utilities costs; storage of goods.

Assessment

  • Construction and remodelling are necessary to bring the store to the condition necessary for it to be capable of operating as intended — capitalise.
  • The supermarket cannot open without the remodelling expenditure.
  • Utilities and storage of goods are operating expenditures — not necessary to bring the store to that condition — expense.
  • Salaries of staff directly related to construction and remodelling need careful analysis (may capitalise the directly attributable portion).

Conclusion

  • Capitalise construction / remodelling (and directly attributable staff costs linked to that work).
  • Expense utilities and storage of goods.
  • Apply the “capable of operating as intended” test item-by-item to the budget line items.

Illustration: 3.2.4 — Land clearing and grading capitalised to landDTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.2.1.1-1 IAS 16 · para 16 IAS 16 · para 17(b) IAS 16 · para 59

Facts

  • A ski-slope operator develops land into a ski resort.
  • As part of development, the operator cuts down trees, clears and grades the land and hills, and constructs ski lifts.

Assessment

  • Tree cutting, land clearing and grading are site-preparation costs directly attributable to bringing the land to working condition for its intended use.
  • Those costs are part of the cost of the land, not the ski lifts.
  • When site dismantlement / removal / restoration cost is included in the cost of land, that portion of the land asset is depreciated over the period of benefits obtained by incurring those costs (IAS 16 · para 59).

Conclusion

  • Capitalise clearing and grading to land.
  • Capitalise ski-lift construction to the ski-lift asset.
  • Depreciate any restoration component embedded in land cost over the benefit period.

Illustration: 3.2.5 — Capitalising ROU depreciation during leasehold constructionKPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · Example 1B IAS 16 · para 10 IAS 16 · para 16(b) DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.2.2.1-2 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 4.1.1

Facts

  • Company B leases land with several buildings in shell form (interior and infrastructure not yet completed); lease term 20 years.
  • Management intends to use the premises as corporate headquarters.
  • Significant leasehold improvements are required; over 10 months B constructs interiors (ceilings, frames, electricity, lighting, air conditioning, work stations).
  • Contrast case (KPMG 3.2.30.80): retailer rents retail space that could open immediately, but first refurbishes for three months for branding — ROU depreciation during that refurbishment is treated as a cost of opening a new facility and expensed.

Assessment

  • Constructing leasehold improvements in shell buildings is similar to constructing a building.
  • ROU depreciation during the construction period is directly attributable to bringing the premises to the condition necessary to operate as headquarters.
  • IAS 16 · para 10 expressly contemplates ROU depreciation entering PPE cost when the leased asset is used to construct / add to / replace / service PPE.
  • Opening-a-new-facility costs (IAS 16 · para 19(a)) are not PPE cost — hence the retailer contrast.

Conclusion

  • Capitalise ROU depreciation during the 10-month construction of headquarters leasehold improvements into the cost of those improvements / building.
  • Expense ROU depreciation during a mere branding refurbishment of space that could already open (opening-facility cost).
  • Judgement turns on whether the leased asset is being used to bring PPE to intended operating condition versus opening a new facility.

Illustration: 3.2.6 — Customer reimbursement of transformation cost is revenue, not a PPE-cost reductionDTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.2.6.1-1 IFRS 15 IAS 16 · para 16

Facts

  • Company A contracts with Company B to produce and sell a specific product.
  • To produce it, Company A must transform a major part of its plant and commissions that work from Company C, unconnected with A and B.
  • The transformation costs are significant and exist only for this sales contract.
  • A and B agree that B will reimburse A for a portion of those costs. The cash from B may be used only to offset the transformation.
  • From A’s perspective two transactions occur: the purchase of transformation services from C, and the supply of products to B.

Assessment

  • Decide whether the amount receivable from B belongs to the purchase from C or to the supply to B.
  • The arrangement between A and B is within IFRS 15. B contracts only with A, so the transaction price includes all amounts B pays to A.
  • The use-restriction on the cash does not change that analysis. The cash buys services that create assets A controls and uses only to supply B, and it is still revenue.
  • On receipt, defer the reimbursement and recognise it later as revenue under IFRS 15 for that contract.
  • Recognise the transformation separately as PPE, with no offset of the amount received from B.

Conclusion

  • PPE cost is the full transformation cost.
  • The restricted cash from B is not a reduction of that cost.
  • Revenue timing for the reimbursement stays in IFRS 15.
  • A use-restriction is not a cost contribution to the plant.
  • A customer’s contribution of an asset itself, rather than this cash reimbursement, is in 3.1A.

Illustration: 3.2.7 — Broker rebates seller-paid commission to the property purchaserDTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.2.7-1 IAS 16 · para 16

Facts

  • During negotiations to purchase a property, the purchaser will not accept the seller’s best offer.
  • To induce the purchaser to proceed, the broker rebates a portion of the seller-paid commission to the purchaser.
  • The commission was paid by the seller, not by the purchaser.
  • From the purchaser’s perspective only one transaction occurs: the purchase of the property.
  • The seller’s best offer is not the price the purchaser actually pays.

Assessment

  • The commission rebate is not immediate income to the purchaser.
  • If the purchaser had paid a brokerage commission, that commission would be part of the cost of the property, as a cost necessarily incurred to obtain the asset.
  • The rebate is analysed the same way: it is a component of the cost of the property.
  • Paragraph 16(a) already takes trade discounts and rebates into the purchase-price limb. This rebate follows that cost analysis rather than a separate income entry.
  • Do not recognise the rebate in profit or loss on the purchase date.

Conclusion

  • Treat the rebate as a component of property cost.
  • Do not recognise immediate income.
  • The price the purchaser actually bears, after the rebate, is the cost.
  • There is one transaction, not a purchase plus a commission-income event.
  • Contrast Illustration 3.2.6, where a customer’s reimbursement of transformation is revenue because it sits in a supply contract’s transaction price.

Illustration: 3.2.8 — Capacity fee: transferable intangible or attached building costDTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.2.8-1 IAS 16 · para 16 IAS 38

Facts

  • In some jurisdictions, developers of factories, offices, apartment buildings and shopping malls pay a capacity fee to the government.
  • The fee is the privilege of purchasing, on an ongoing basis, defined quantities of electricity and other utilities beyond minimum quantities that can be bought without the fee.
  • The fee is paid once. It covers supply either indefinitely or for a substantial number of years.
  • The building owner still pays the market rate for the electricity and other utilities actually purchased.
  • The fee attaches to the entity that owns the building. It can be transferred to another building if the capacity allowed by the fee is not fully used.

Assessment

  • Because the fee can move between buildings, it is not part of the cost of the building.
  • Recognise it as a purchased intangible asset.
  • That intangible follows IAS 38 for amortisation and impairment.
  • If instead the fee were attached to the building and could not be transferred, recognise it as part of the cost of the building and depreciate it with the building.
  • Ongoing purchases at the market rate are period costs. They are not the capacity fee.

Conclusion

  • Transferable capacity fee: IAS 38 intangible, not PPE.
  • Non-transferable fee attached to the building: PPE cost, depreciated.
  • Do not force a transferable fee into building cost.
  • Both branches are required. The split is whether the fee can move to another building.
  • IAS 38 amortisation and impairment of the transferable fee are not worked in this chapter.

Illustration: 3.2.9 — Capitalise customary costs between physical completion and occupation approvalDTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.2.9-2 IAS 16 · para 16(b) IAS 16 · para 22 IAS 23 · para 25

Facts

  • On 20 September 20X0 Company A finishes a building intended as its administrative headquarters.
  • By law, the local health and safety authority must approve the offices for occupation before any activity can start.
  • Approval can be requested only when the building is physically complete. It takes on average three months.
  • The authority issues the approval on 20 December 20X0.
  • In those three months Company A incurs CU10 of building-management costs (utility and security) and interest. The building is an IAS 23 qualifying asset.

Assessment

  • Costs between physical completion and approval are included in the initial cost of the building.
  • The management costs are directly attributable to bringing the asset to the location and condition necessary for it to be capable of operating in the manner intended by management.
  • Obtaining occupation approval is an activity necessary to prepare the qualifying asset for its intended use, so borrowing costs continue until approval.
  • Abnormal amounts of wasted resources incurred in obtaining that approval are not capitalised.
  • If approval takes longer than the customary three months because Company A fails to provide the required information, capitalise only the costs of the usual three-month period. If the delay is the authority’s slow response, and Company A did not cause it, extend the capitalisation period.

Conclusion

  • Capitalise the CU10 management costs and the interest incurred in the customary three months.
  • Physical completion alone does not stop capitalisation while lawful occupation still needs approval.
  • Avoidable delay caused by the entity: only the customary window enters cost.
  • Authority delay without fault by the entity: the capitalisation period is extended.
  • Use CU10 as stated. Abnormal waste stays out of cost.

3.6 Self-constructed assetstop

The cost of a self-constructed asset is determined using the same principles as for an acquired asset. If an entity makes similar assets for sale in the normal course of business, the cost of the asset is usually the same as the cost of constructing an asset for sale (IAS 2). Any internal profits are eliminated. The cost of abnormal amounts of wasted material, labour or other resources is not included. IAS 23 establishes criteria for recognising interest as a component of the carrying amount of a self-constructed qualifying asset. IAS 16 · para 22 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.23 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.2.2 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 4.1.5 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.40.10

  • Similar assets for sale → IAS 2 cost. Example (DTT): a car manufacturer may use IAS 2 inventory-construction cost to measure a car held as PPE. If the entity does not construct similar assets for sale, only IAS 16 · para 16 elements enter cost — direct materials, direct labour, and unavoidable directly attributable costs (costs that would have been avoided if the asset had not been constructed). Systematic allocation of fixed production overheads (IAS 2 conversion style) is not appropriate under IAS 16. DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.2.2.1-1 IAS 16 · para 22
  • Eliminate internal profits. Internal profits are eliminated in arriving at self-constructed cost. IAS 16 · para 22
  • Abnormal waste excluded. Abnormal wasted material, labour or other resources are expensed. Judgement considers technical difficulty, scale, planned timelines and the usual construction process for that asset type. Principles also apply to installation necessary to bring a purchased asset to working condition. IAS 16 · para 22 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.40.10
  • Demolition of pre-existing structures. If structures were acquired as part of the site with the specific intention of demolishing them, demolition costs are incremental and enter the cost of the new asset / land as appropriate. If the entity previously used the building in its business, write off the old building’s carrying amount (revise useful life / residual to demolition date) and expense demolition costs as part of derecognition — do not capitalise into the new building (see Illustration 3.6.2).   • Cancellation fees (KPMG). A fee paid to cancel an existing supply contract in order to buy cheaper machines elsewhere is not directly attributable to the machines acquired — expense when incurred. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · Example 1F IAS 16 · para 16(b)

IAS 16 · para 22A

3.7 Cash-price equivalent / deferred paymenttop

The cost of an item of PPE is the cash price equivalent at the recognition date. If payment is deferred beyond normal credit terms, the difference between the cash price equivalent and the total payment is recognised as interest over the period of credit unless such interest is capitalised in accordance with IAS 23. IAS 16 · para 23 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.34 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.3.2 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 4.1.6 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.130.10

  • Cash price equivalent, not IFRS 9 market-rate discounting by default (KPMG). IAS 16 specifically addresses deferred payment for PPE: cost is the current cash price at recognition. That calculation may differ from IFRS 9’s market-rate discounting of the financial liability; apply IAS 16 for the asset’s cost. Interest difference is recognised over the credit period (effective interest method in KPMG’s view) unless capitalised under IAS 23. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.2 · 3.2.130.10 IAS 16 · para 23
  • “Normal credit terms” (DTT). Settlement that takes a few days, weeks or even months depending on industry and national law does not require interest imputation. A multi-year interest-free period advertised as an inducement is not normal credit terms — impute interest so the asset is recognised at current cash sale price. DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.3.2-2
  • Large up-front deposit (DTT). Where a non-refundable deposit is paid years before delivery and the total payable is reduced for time value, unwind the deposit’s time value into the cost of the asset using the rate implicit in the transaction. DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.3.2-3 IAS 16 · para 23

3.7A Variable / contingent consideration on PPE purchasetop

A purchase of PPE may be an initial payment plus further amounts that are contingent on a future event, outcome, or sale of the acquired asset at a threshold price. IAS 16 does not choose a single method. Two approaches are observed when the future payment relates to the cost of the asset. Both are acceptable. The policy is applied consistently to similar transactions and disclosed when material. PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.32 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.33 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 4.1.9

  • What the contingency can depend on. The buyer is usually obliged, by contract or by statute, to pay the additional amount if the future event or condition occurs. EY’s examples of variability are an index or a rate, the purchaser’s future actions, the condition of the asset, and the asset’s performance. One contract may contain more than one source. Arrangements that are akin to profit-sharing may sit outside any Standard. PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.32 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 4.1.9
  • First test: is the future payment related to the cost of the asset? Only after that analysis does the choice of approach arise. A payment that is not a cost of the asset is not forced into PPE. PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.33
  • IFRS Interpretations Committee — too broad for one answer. The IFRS IC discussed variable payments for an asset purchase between 2011 and 2016, including accounting at the purchase date and subsequently, and it distinguished payments that depend on the purchaser’s future activity from those that do not. The agenda decision (March 2016) determined that the issue was too broad to address within existing Standards and recommended that the IASB address variable payments comprehensively. No Standard selects one of the two approaches below. PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.32 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 4.1.9
  • When the liability is recognised is itself a policy. Read the contract: whether the payments are linked to the initial value of the asset or to its future performance; whether the contingency is inside or outside the purchaser’s control; and whether the variable amount is payment for some other good or service from the seller. Some contracts establish the obligation at the outset. Others are executory, and the liability is recognised only when a party performs. Where the contingency is outside the control of both parties, IAS 32 · para 25 treats a contingent obligation to deliver cash as a financial liability. EY records that this reading was discussed by the IFRS IC and was not confirmed in the final agenda decision. Develop the policy under the IAS 8 hierarchy so the information is relevant and reliable. Dissimilar payments may use different policies. Disclose the policy when it is material. EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 4.1.9 IAS 32 · para 25

3.8 Exchanges of assetstop

One or more items of PPE may be acquired in exchange for a non-monetary asset or assets, or a combination of monetary and non-monetary assets. The cost of the item acquired is measured at fair value unless (a) the exchange lacks commercial substance or (b) the fair value of neither the asset received nor the asset given up is reliably measurable. If not measured at fair value, cost is measured at the carrying amount of the asset given up. IAS 16 · para 24 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · 22.37 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.3.3 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 4.4

  • Commercial substance (para 25). An exchange has commercial substance if:
    • (a) the configuration (risk, timing and amount) of the cash flows of the asset received differs from that of the asset transferred; or
    • (b) the entity-specific value of the portion of the entity’s operations affected by the transaction changes as a result of the exchange; and
    • (c) the difference in (a) or (b) is significant relative to the fair value of the assets exchanged.
    Entity-specific value for this test reflects post-tax cash flows. The result may be clear without detailed calculations. IAS 16 · para 25 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 4.4.1 PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.38.1
  • Reliably measurable fair value (para 26). Fair value is reliably measurable if (a) variability in the range of reasonable fair value measurements is not significant, or (b) the probabilities of the various estimates within the range can be reasonably assessed and used. If either asset’s FV is reliably measurable, use FV of the asset given up unless FV of the asset received is more clearly evident. IAS 16 · para 26 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · section 4.4.2
  • Gain recognition does not depend on dissimilar assets (BC19). The acquired item is measured at FV even if the entity cannot immediately derecognise the asset given up. Gains on derecognition go to P&L but are not classified as revenue (except certain assets previously held for rental — Chapter 6). IAS 16 · para 24 IAS 16 · para BC19 IAS 16 · para 68
  • Mixed consideration (PwC). Entity exchanges surplus land (book CU100,000) for cash CU200,000 and plant valued at CU250,000; transaction has commercial substance → plant recorded at CU250,000 (= FV of land CU450,000 − cash received CU200,000). PwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.37.1 IAS 16 · para 24

Illustration: 3.8.1 — Exchange with commercial substance measured at fair valueEY iGAAP 2026 Ch19 · Illustration 4-3 DTT iGAAP 2022 A7 · 4.3.3-1 IAS 16 · para 24 IAS 16 · para 25

Facts (EY Illustration 4-3)

  • Entity holds land and office buildings: aggregate carrying amount CU600,000; fair value CU750,000.
  • Exchanges them for land and buildings (a manufacturing facility) in another location with fair value CU1,000,000, and pays additional cash CU250,000.
  • The transaction has commercial substance.
  • Parallel DTT pattern (different CU): ship charterer exchanges land and buildings (CA CU10m; FV CU15m) for a ship (FV CU18m) plus cash CU3m — same measurement logic.

Measurement

  • Cost of asset received = FV of asset received = CU1,000,000 (commercial substance + FV reliably measurable).
  • Gain on disposal = FV received − CA given up − cash paid = 1,000,000 − 600,000 − 250,000 = CU150,000.
  • DTT ship parallel: recognise ship at CU18m; profit = 18m − 10m − 3m = CU5m.

Conclusion

  • Measure acquired PPE at fair value when the exchange has commercial substance and FV is reliably measurable.
  • Recognise gain in profit or loss (not as revenue).
  • If commercial substance fails or neither FV is reliably measurable → measure at CA of asset given up (see Illustration 3.8.2).

How is PPE cost measured in a commercial-substance asset exchange?

Illustration: 3.8.2 — Exchange lacking commercial substance: measure at carrying amount given upPwC MOA 2020 Ch22 · FAQ 22.39.1 IAS 16 · para 24 IAS 16 · para 25

Facts

  • Entity A exchanges car X (book value C13,000; fair value C13,250) for cash of C150 and car Y (fair value C13,100).
  • The entity’s cash flows are not expected to change as a result of the exchange — it is in substantially the same position as before.
  • The transaction lacks commercial substance.

Assessment

  • Without commercial substance, IAS 16 · para 24 requires measurement at the carrying amount of the asset given up — not at fair value.
  • Allocate the given-up CA between cash received and the asset received.

Conclusion

  • Recognise cash C150.
  • Recognise car Y as PPE at carrying value C12,850 (= C13,000 − C150).
  • No fair-value gain is recognised on the exchange.

3.10 IAS 23 borrowing-costs see-also tiptop

Borrowing costs on qualifying assets. Borrowing costs that are directly attributable to the acquisition, construction or production of a qualifying asset are capitalised as part of the cost of that asset under IAS 23. A qualifying asset necessarily takes a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended use or sale. IAS 16 · para 22 points to IAS 23 for interest on self-constructed PPE. Unwinding of a decommissioning discount is not a borrowing cost under IAS 23. This chapter does not deep-dive IAS 23 (commencement, suspension, cessation, specific vs general borrowings).
