Chapter 2 — Recognition

Concept Map

2.0 Recognition of inventoriestop

An entity initially recognises inventory when it has control of the inventory, expects it to provide future economic benefits, and the cost of the inventory can be measured reliably; IAS 2 itself contains no specific guidance on the timing of recognition. Recognition starts with the control, benefits and reliable-cost tests, then timing when IAS 2 itself gives no date test, measurement at cost on initial recognition, and recognition as an expense under paragraphs 34–35. PwC MOA 2020 · 25.9 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.90

  • Initial recognition criteria. An entity should initially recognise inventory when:
    • It has control of the inventory
    • It expects the inventory to provide future economic benefits
    • The cost of the inventory can be measured reliably
    Inventories comprise assets:
    • Held for sale in the ordinary course of business
    • In the process of production for such sale
    • In the form of materials or supplies to be consumed in the production process or in the rendering of services
    The definition and its boundaries are in Chapter 1 · 1.1. PwC MOA 2020 · 25.9 Conceptual Framework · para 4.3 Conceptual Framework · para 4.4 Conceptual Framework · para BC5.1 IAS 2 · para 6
  • Timing. IAS 2 does not include specific guidance on the timing of recognition of purchased inventories. In the absence of that guidance, entities generally refer to the revenue recognition requirements: inventory is recognised on the date on which the entity obtains control of it (2.2). KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.90 IFRS 15 · para 31
  • Measurement on initial recognition. Initial measurement of inventories is at cost.
    • All costs of purchase, costs of conversion and other costs incurred in bringing the inventories to their present location and condition, assigned to items using the cost formulas (Chapter 3)
    • After initial recognition, inventories are measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value (Chapter 4)
    IAS 2 · para 9 IAS 2 · para 10 IAS 2 · para 23–27 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.15
  • Recognition as an expense. The only IAS 2 paragraphs headed as recognition deal with when the carrying amount of inventories is recognised as an expense — paragraphs 34–35 — on sale, on write-down or loss, on reversal of a write-down, and when inventories are allocated to another asset (Chapter 5). IAS 2 · para 34 IAS 2 · para 35

2.1 Items recognised as inventoriestop

Items recognised as inventories. Inventories include:

  • Goods purchased and held for resale — merchandise purchased by a retailer, or land and other property held for resale
  • The materials, work in progress and finished goods of a manufacturer
  • Items that do not meet the definition of property, plant and equipment, such as certain spare parts, are treated as inventory

2.2 Timing of recognition — controltop

Inventory is recognised on the date on which the entity obtains control of it; the indicators of control are evaluated together, and shipping terms inform — but do not alone determine — when control passes. Timing turns on whether the entity controls the inventory, which indicators show that control has passed, what shipping terms add without deciding alone, whether an intermediary is principal or agent, and what remains inventory at the reporting date. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.90 IFRS 15 · para 31

  • Control. An entity has control of inventory when it is able to direct its use and obtain substantially all of its remaining benefits.
    • This may occur at a point in time or over time.
    • Control includes the ability to prevent other entities from directing the use of, and obtaining the benefits from, the asset.
    KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.90 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.45 IFRS 15 · para 33
  • Indicators that control has passed. Indicators that control has passed include
    • The entity having a present obligation to pay
    • Physical possession
    • Legal title
    • The risks and rewards of ownership
    • Accepted the asset
    They are factors often present when an entity has control of inventory, but they are not individually determinative, nor a list of conditions that have to be met — the entity evaluates all available evidence. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.90 IFRS 15 · para 38
  • Shipping terms. Shipping terms — free on board (FOB) — alone do not determine when control transfers; they are considered with the other indicators.
    • They often indicate when the entity has legal title, the risks and rewards of ownership and a present obligation to pay.
    • When goods are shipped FOB, an entity usually receives the bill of lading and takes over the risk of loss or damage when the goods are loaded onto the ship — which may indicate that it obtains control when the goods are loaded at the seller’s port.
    • If so, inventory includes items bought FOB that are in transit at the reporting date.
    KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.90
  • Principal or agent. An intermediary determines whether it acts as a principal or an agent — whether it controls the inventory before it is transferred to the customer — first by applying the general guidance on the transfer of control and, if that is not conclusive, the specific principal versus agent indicators. An intermediary recognises inventory only as a principal.
    Principal or agentInventory
    PrincipalRecognises the inventory in its statement of financial position

Because derecognition depends on the timing of revenue recognition, inventory does not include items sold, even if a normal level of returns is expected, or items shipped to customers on or before the reporting date unless control over those items has not transferred to the buyer.

2.3 Consignment stock and reservation of titletop

Consignment arrangements, sale and repurchase transactions and reservation-of-title clauses require careful consideration of whether control — not legal title or physical delivery alone — has passed. Consignment and title clauses walk through delivery where the seller retains control, the indicators of a consignment arrangement, how consignor and consignee each account, the distributor’s view of when risks pass, arrangements beyond distribution such as outsourced repackaging, and reservation of title. PwC MOA 2020 · 25.46 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.100

  • Seller delivers, but retains control. A seller may deliver goods to another party, such as a dealer or distributor for sale to an end customer, but retain control of them. Arrangements of this kind are common in certain industries, particularly the motor vehicle trade: the manufacturer (or a financier) generally retains title until a predetermined event occurs.
    • The dealer sells the goods
    • The dealer has held them for a set period
    • The dealer pays for and adopts them
    Title tends to pass some time after physical transfer, generally on receipt of cleared funds (but not to the dealer if it has already sold the vehicle on). The key issue is the point at which the dealer has, in substance, acquired an asset to recognise in its statement of financial position — when legal title passes, or at some other time. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.100 PwC MOA 2020 · FAQ 25.46.1
  • Indicators of a consignment arrangement. Not a closed list.
    • The goods are controlled by the seller until a specified event occurs, such as sale to a customer of the intermediary, or until a specified period expires
    • The seller is able to require the return of the goods or transfer them to a third party, such as another dealer
    • The intermediary does not have an unconditional obligation to pay for the goods, although it might be required to pay a deposit
    A product delivered to another party that is held on consignment does not give rise to revenue on delivery — and so the manufacturer does not derecognise the inventory on delivery. IFRS 15 · para B77 IFRS 15 · para B78 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.100 PwC MOA 2020 · FAQ 25.46.1 DTT A11 2022 · 2.4-1
  • Consignor and consignee.
    CaseTreatment
    Consignor Items controlled by an entity that are held on consignment at another entity’s premises are included in the inventory of the consignor. The consignor continues to account for consignment stock until control has passed — including material stock at a third party’s premises, for disclosure judgement.
    Consignee Items held on consignment on behalf of another entity are not included in the inventory of the consignee.
    KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.100 EY IGAAP 2026 · Ch23 · section 2.3.1.H
  • Distributor’s view. Goods remain the manufacturer’s inventories until the transfer is substantive.
    • Where the manufacturer retains the substantial risks and rewards of ownership and legal title until a predetermined event (for example, sale to a third-party customer), the distributor determines when to recognise the goods as an asset.
    • IFRS Accounting Standards do not provide guidance from the buyer’s point of view, so under IAS 8 the distributor determines a treatment that results in relevant and reliable information.
    • Until the transfer to the distributor is established as substantive, the goods are treated as the manufacturer’s inventories and excluded from the distributor’s statement of financial position.
    DTT A11 2022 · 2.4-1 IAS 2 · para 6
  • Arrangements beyond distribution. Consignment-type questions also arise outside distribution — for example, outsourced repackaging in the pharmaceutical sector that takes the legal form of a sale and then a repurchase — and are resolved by the same control analysis. Sale and repurchase arrangements are revenue questions under IFRS 15. EY IGAAP 2026 · Ch23 · section 2.3.1.H PwC MOA 2020 · 25.46
  • Reservation of title. Legal title is one of five indicators of control.
    • In some jurisdictions sellers commonly include reservation-of-title clauses so that they retain legal ownership of the goods until the purchaser has paid.
    • The main effect is to improve the unpaid seller’s position if the purchaser becomes insolvent, and whether an effective reservation exists depends on the construction of the particular contract.
    • Under IFRS 15 the sale is recognised when the purchaser obtains control; passage of title is one of five indicators, and the entity considers all of them.
    • The seller recognises the product as inventory — and the customer does not — until the customer has obtained control.
    PwC MOA 2020 · FAQ 25.46.2 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.46 IFRS 15 · para 38

Facts

  • Manufacturer provides household products to Retailer on a consignment basis.
  • Retailer does not take title until the products are scanned at the register.
  • Retailer has no obligation to pay Manufacturer until the products are sold to the consumer, unless they are lost or damaged while in Retailer’s possession.
  • Unsold products, excluding those lost or damaged, can be returned; Manufacturer has discretion to call products back or transfer them to another customer.

Control

  • Control has not transferred to Retailer while Manufacturer is able to require the return or transfer of the products.
  • The products are held on consignment, so Manufacturer recognises no revenue on delivery to Retailer.

Manufacturer’s inventory

  • The goods are treated as Manufacturer’s inventory until control transfers to Retailer.
  • Manufacturer derecognises the inventory when the products are sold to the consumer, or when they are lost or damaged while in Retailer’s possession.
  • Retailer, as consignee, does not include the products in its inventory.

2.4 Derecognition and recognition as an expensetop

Inventory is derecognised only when control of the asset is transferred to the customer; its carrying amount is then recognised as an expense in the period in which the related revenue is recognised, unless it forms part of the cost of another asset. How much to recognise as an expense, the removal of matching-principle language, and when inventory is allocated into another asset such as self-constructed PPE are developed in Chapter 5. Key terms

  • Initial recognition of inventory — when the entity has control of the inventory, expects it to provide future economic benefits, and its cost can be measured reliably
  • Control of inventory — the ability to direct its use and obtain substantially all of its remaining benefits (IFRS 15 para 33)
  • Indicators that control has passed — present obligation to pay, physical possession, legal title, risks and rewards of ownership, acceptance; not individually determinative (IFRS 15 para 38)
  • Principal or agent — an intermediary recognises inventory only if it controls the goods before they are transferred to the customer
  • Consignment arrangement — goods delivered to another party that has not obtained control of them (IFRS 15 paras B77–B78)
  • Reservation of title — seller retains legal ownership until payment; one of five indicators of control, not decisive alone
