3.0 Measurement of inventoriestop

Inventories are measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV); initial measurement is at cost — all costs of purchase, costs of conversion and other costs incurred in bringing the inventories to their present location and condition — assigned to items by specific identification or by a cost formula. Inventories are measured at the lower of cost and NRV, subject to the producer and broker-trader exceptions. IAS 2 · para 9 IAS 2 · para 10 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.15 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.110

  • Lower of cost and NRV. Inventories shall be measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value.

3.2 Costs of purchasetop

The costs of purchase comprise the purchase price, import duties and other non-recoverable taxes, and transport, handling and other directly attributable costs. Purchase cost starts from the purchase price plus non-recoverable duties, taxes, transport and handling, less discounts; sales tax enters only when it is not recoverable; a single price for several assets is allocated on relative fair values; exchange differences on foreign-currency invoices stay out of purchase cost; and normal material wastage is product cost while abnormal wastage is expensed. Facts

  • Retailer Entity A acquires products from manufacturers and sells them to end users.
  • The manufacturers grant incentives to A on one of two bases: (i) a 10% prompt settlement discount on all purchases of inventories settled within 30 days of purchase; or (ii) rebates based on the volume of merchandise purchased or sold.
  • The accounting depends on whether A acts as principal or as agent in the purchase and sale (principal versus agent: Chapter 2 · 2.2).

Entity A acts as principal

  • Rebates and discounts received as a reduction in the purchase price of inventories are taken into consideration in measuring the cost of those inventories (para 11).
  • Prompt settlement (cash) discount: deducted from the cost of the inventories; A estimates the expected settlement discount to be received from the supplier when measuring cost.
  • Volume rebates: similarly deducted from the cost of inventories — when the rebate is recognised: Illustration 3.2.2.

Entity A acts as agent

  • If A is not purchasing the products as principal, and instead acts as an agent of the manufacturers in making sales to end users, any rebate received forms part of the net commission revenue earned.
  • The rebate is not a reduction of the cost of inventories — an agent does not recognise the goods as its inventories.

Facts

  • 1 July 20X1: in a binding arrangement, a vendor offers a cash refund of currency units (CU) 1,000 to a customer if, during the 12 months to 30 June 20X2, the customer purchases 1,000 units of a particular product.
  • Historically the customer has purchased, on average, 1,700 units each year; there have been no significant changes in the trading relationship in the current period.
  • At the year end, 31 December 20X1, the 1,000-unit threshold has not yet been reached; the customer anticipates reaching it before 30 June 20X2.
  • The cash refund is a volume rebate — a reduction in the cost of inventories (para 11). Para 11 does not specify the treatment of refunds anticipated but not yet earned, or their allocation to items of inventory.

Anticipation of a refund not yet earned

  • IAS 34 addresses rebates anticipated but not yet earned in full in interim financial statements; it is appropriate to apply that principle to annual financial statements.
  • IAS 34 para B23: volume rebates and other contractual changes in prices are anticipated, by both payer and recipient, if it is probable that they have been earned or will take effect.
  • Discretionary rebates and discounts are not anticipated — the resulting asset or liability would not satisfy the Conceptual Framework conditions (the reference is to the 2010 Conceptual Framework).
  • If it is probable that the customer will purchase at least 1,000 units in the period (for example, forecast purchases are in line with historical volumes), the contractual refund is accrued as the units are purchased.

Units to which the refund is allocated

  • The refund is allocated to the units purchased on a systematic and rational basis.
  • One-off incentive, not expected to be repeated: it may be appropriate to reduce the cost of the first 1,000 units purchased — entitlement is based only on the purchase of 1,000 units.
  • Routine annual offer conditional on the customer buying all inventories of this type from the vendor (exclusive supply): it may be more appropriate to treat the refund as relating to all purchases in the year, reducing the cost of all units purchased.
  • The assessment is made on the specific facts and circumstances, including all relevant contractual terms.

Facts

  • Company Z, a furniture retailer, buys beds from Company Y at a cost of 100 each.
  • Y has agreed to grant Z a 10% refund on all purchases if Z buys at least 10,000 beds in a 12-month period.

Rebate probable from the outset (Example 4A)

  • Based on past experience, it is probable that Z will buy 10,000 beds from Y.
  • Z records the beds at the expected cost of 90 per unit.
  • Z recognises a receivable for the anticipated rebate.

Rebate becomes probable after nine months (Example 4B)

  • Initially it was not probable that Z would buy 10,000 beds, so each bed was recorded at the gross unit cost of 100.
  • After nine months Z has bought 9,000 beds and concludes that it is now probable that it will meet the minimum and receive the 10% rebate; 3,000 beds are still on hand and 6,000 have been sold.
  • Z reduces the cost of each remaining bed by 10 — 30,000 (3,000 × 10) reduces the cost of inventory.
  • The remaining 60,000 is recognised in profit or loss immediately as a reduction of cost of sales.

Facts

  • A car dealer receives bonuses from car manufacturers when it reaches certain sales targets of motor vehicles.
  • The sales-related bonuses are received only after the cars have been sold — that is, when they are no longer in inventory.
  • Question: when is a bonus related to the sale of inventory recognised?

Recognition of the bonus

  • The bonus from the manufacturers is a contingent asset.
  • It is recognised only when it becomes virtually certain.

Illustration: 3.2.6 — Import duties on cars held in a customs-free zonePwC MOA 2020 · FAQ 25.19.2 IAS 2 · para 10 IAS 2 · para 11

Facts

  • A car dealer imports cars, bringing them initially into a customs-free zone.
  • The cars remain there until they are sold and delivered to customers, at which point import duties become payable by the importer.
  • Question: are the estimated import duties on inventory held in the customs-free zone included in the cost of inventories?

Inventory held in the customs-free zone

  • Import duties are not included in the inventory valuation for inventory held in a customs-free zone.
  • Import duties do not arise from shipping the cars to the customs-free zone, so they are not included in the inventory valuation at that point.

When the cars leave the zone

  • Import duties payable are included in the cost of inventory when the cars leave the customs-free zone.
  • The duties are a cost of purchase (para 11) incurred in bringing the cars to their present location and condition (para 10) only from that point.

Variable payments for the purchase of inventories

Where the consideration payable for inventory varies with a market price, with the physical attributes of the goods delivered, or with both, the payable is within the scope of IFRS 9; the source of the variability determines whether an embedded derivative is separated and how later changes affect the cost of inventory. IFRS is not clear on the appropriate accounting for variable payments relating to the purchase of inventory, so the market-price element gives rise to an accounting policy choice, applied consistently; whichever policy is chosen, the inventory is measured at the lower of cost and NRV. Facts

  • Entity A purchases commodity X on 31 January 20X1; it is delivered and control is transferred at that date. A classifies commodity X as inventory.
  • The purchase price is based on the spot commodity price at 30 April 20X1, and the consideration is payable at that date.
  • There is no other variability associated with the consideration.

Initial recognition and embedded derivative

  • Commodity X is recognised as inventory on delivery at 31 January 20X1, based on the forward commodity price for 30 April 20X1 at 31 January 20X1.
  • The variability in the payable associated with the commodity price gives rise to an embedded derivative that is not closely related to the host financial instrument; IFRS 9 requires such embedded derivatives to be separated and accounted for as derivatives if they meet the stated criteria.
  • The embedded derivative is therefore separated from the short-term host payable unless the payable is designated at FVPL in its entirety.

Subsequent changes in fair value

  • IFRS is not clear on the accounting for variable payments relating to the purchase of inventory, so A has an accounting policy choice for subsequent changes in the fair value of the embedded derivative:
  • (i) Adjust the cost of inventory under IAS 2 paras 10–11 — the changes are part of the purchase and other costs incurred in bringing the inventory to its present location and condition; or (ii) Profit or loss under IFRS 9 para 5.7.1 — the cost of inventory is determined at delivery and the bifurcated derivative is accounted for as if freestanding.
  • The chosen policy is applied consistently; regardless of the policy, the inventory is measured at the lower of cost and NRV (para 9).

Commodity broker-trader at fair value less costs to sell

  • Commodity broker-traders that measure inventory at fair value less costs to sell are outside the measurement requirements of IAS 2 (para 3(b); Chapter 1 · 1.3).
  • Their forward contracts to buy inventory are typically IFRS 9 derivatives — the own-use (normal purchase) exemption is not met.
  • On physical settlement, the inventory likewise stays at fair value less costs to sell.

Contract accounted for as a derivative — physical settlement

  • If the entity does not measure inventory at fair value less costs to sell, IAS 2 lower of cost and NRV applies.

3.9 Techniques for the measurement of costtop

Techniques for the measurement of the cost of inventories, such as the standard cost method or the retail method, may be used for convenience if the results approximate cost. Convenience techniques compared here are the standard cost method, the retail method, and the base stock method used only when it approximates IFRS.   • Standard cost method Standard costs take into account normal levels of materials and supplies, labour, efficiency and capacity utilisation. They are regularly reviewed and, if necessary, revised in the light of current conditions. Under a standard costing system the cost of a product is determined using predetermined rates for material, labour and overhead expenses based on manufacturing specifications; the method is often used by manufacturing entities to allocate fixed and variable production overheads to each item produced, with the standard cost based on budgeted fixed production overheads and the normal capacity of the production facilities. Standard costs are reviewed regularly — at least at each reporting date — and are used for year-end inventory valuation only if they relate to actual costs incurred during the period, achieved by frequent updating of standards or by adjusting for the recorded variances. If standard costs are not updated to approximate actual costs, the variance accounts are analysed and part of the variances apportioned to inventory (Illustration 3.9.3).   • Retail method — sales value reduced by the appropriate percentage gross margin. The retail method is often used in the retail industry for measuring inventories of large numbers of rapidly changing items with similar margins for which it is impracticable to use other costing methods. The cost of the inventory is determined by reducing the sales value of the inventory by the appropriate percentage gross margin. The percentage used takes into consideration inventory that has been marked down to below its original selling price. An average percentage for each retail department is often used. The average margins take into account reductions from the original selling price due to sales or other promotions; an average percentage for each retail department may be used if the margins on all products within the department are similar. The method may be used if the result approximates actual cost, reviewed at least at each reporting date (Illustrations 3.9.1 and 3.9.2). 3.10 Cost formulastop

Cost formulas move from specific identification of individual costs, to when that method is inappropriate for interchangeable items, to FIFO or weighted average for other inventories, how those formulas operate, using the same formula for inventories of similar nature and use, why minimum inventory levels alone do not justify a different formula, and treating a change of formula as a change in accounting policy.

  • Specific identification The cost of inventories of items that are not ordinarily interchangeable and goods or services produced and segregated for specific projects shall be assigned by using specific identification of their individual costs. Specific identification means that specific costs are attributed to identified items of inventory — the appropriate treatment for items segregated for a specific project, whether bought or produced. It is appropriate for unique items, such as custom-built furnishings, property developments, antiques (for example, antique cars) and works of art.   • Specific identification is inappropriate for large numbers of interchangeable items. However, specific identification of costs is inappropriate when there are large numbers of items of inventory that are ordinarily interchangeable. In such circumstances, the method of selecting those items that remain in inventories could be used to obtain predetermined effects on profit or loss. An entity could influence its reported profits by choosing which specific units to sell, and hence which otherwise identical products remain in inventory. Some entities have computer systems that allocate specific costs to ordinarily interchangeable inventories; whether those valuations are acceptable depends on whether they reasonably approximate those that would result from the use of cost formulas. IAS 2 · para 24 DTT A11 2022 · 3.2.8.4
  • FIFO or weighted average for other inventories. The cost of inventories, other than those dealt with in paragraph 23, shall be assigned by using the first-in, first-out (FIFO) or weighted average cost formula. These formulas are used for large numbers of items of inventory that are ordinarily interchangeable (“fungible” items); when specific identification is inappropriate, IAS 2 allows a choice between the two. IAS 2 continues to allow both FIFO and the weighted average methods for interchangeable inventories. IAS 2 · para 25 IAS 2 · para BC21 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.17 DTT A11 2022 · 3.2.8.1 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.280.20
  • How the formulas operate. The FIFO formula assumes that the items of inventory that were purchased or produced first are sold first, and consequently the items remaining in inventory at the end of the period are those most recently purchased or produced. Under the weighted average cost formula, the cost of each item is determined from the weighted average of the cost of similar items at the beginning of a period and the cost of similar items purchased or produced during the period. The average may be calculated on a periodic basis, or as each additional shipment is received, depending upon the circumstances of the entity.

Prohibition of LIFO

LIFO is not permitted; the Board eliminated it because it does not reliably represent inventory flows; arguments for keeping LIFO were considered and rejected; and specific cost methods similar to LIFO remain possible while tax LIFO does not dictate the IFRS formula.

  • LIFO is not permitted. The Standard does not permit the use of the last-in, first-out (LIFO) formula to measure the cost of inventories. The previous version of IAS 2 and SIC-1 allowed some choice between FIFO or weighted average cost formulas (benchmark treatment) and the LIFO method (allowed alternative treatment); the Board decided to eliminate the allowed alternative of using LIFO. Para 25 permits only FIFO and the weighted average cost formula. IAS 2 · para IN13 IAS 2 · para BC9 IAS 2 · para 25 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.17 DTT A11 2022 · 3.2.8.1 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.280.30 EY IGAAP 2026 · Ch23 · section 3.2.2.C
  • Why LIFO was eliminated — not a reliable representation of inventory flows.
    • LIFO treats the newest items of inventory as sold first, so the items remaining are recognised as if they were the oldest — generally not a reliable representation of actual inventory flows.
    • It attempts to meet a perceived deficiency of the conventional model (cost of goods sold measured at outdated prices while revenue is at current prices) by imposing an unrealistic cost flow assumption.
    • Its use is often tax-driven; its effect on profits depends on the relationship between the prices of the most recent acquisitions and replacement cost at the end of the period, so it is not a truly systematic method for determining the effect of changing prices on profits.
    • It results in inventories in the balance sheet at amounts that bear little relationship to recent cost levels, and can distort profit or loss, especially when “preserved” older layers are presumed to have been used when inventories are substantially reduced.

Key terms

  • Cost of inventories — all costs of purchase, costs of conversion and other costs incurred in bringing the inventories to their present location and condition (para 10)
  • Costs of purchase — purchase price, import duties and non-recoverable taxes, transport, handling and other directly attributable costs, less trade discounts, rebates and similar items (para 11)
  • Costs of conversion — costs directly related to the units of production, such as direct labour, plus a systematic allocation of fixed and variable production overheads (para 12)
  • Fixed production overheads — indirect costs of production that remain relatively constant regardless of the volume of production (para 12)
  • Variable production overheads — indirect costs of production that vary directly, or nearly directly, with the volume of production (para 12)
  • Normal capacity — the production expected to be achieved on average over a number of periods or seasons under normal circumstances, taking into account planned maintenance (para 13)
  • Joint products and by-products — more than one product from one production process; conversion costs allocated on a rational and consistent basis (para 14)
  • Financing element — in deferred settlement terms, for example the difference between the purchase price for normal credit terms and the amount paid; recognised as interest expense (para 18)
  • Standard cost method and retail method — techniques for measuring cost, used for convenience if the results approximate cost (paras 21–22)
  • Specific identification — specific costs attributed to identified items; required for items not ordinarily interchangeable and items segregated for specific projects (paras 23–24)
  • FIFO and weighted average — the cost formulas for other inventories; same formula for inventories of similar nature and use (paras 25–27)
  • LIFO — not permitted (para IN13)
