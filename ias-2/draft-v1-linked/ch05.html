Concept Map

5.0 Recognition as an expensetop

Recognised as an expense. The carrying amount of inventories is recognised as an expense when:

  • The inventories are
    • Sold
    • Written down to net realisable value (NRV)
    • Or lost
  • A reversal of a write-down reduces that expense
  • Inventories allocated to another asset are recognised as an expense during the useful life of that asset

5.1 Inventories soldtop

Inventory is derecognised only when control of the asset is transferred to the customer. Sale derecognition ties the expense to related revenue, satisfaction of the performance obligation, transfer of control of the asset, the timing that follows that transfer, consignment sales that may not transfer control, and reservation of title. IAS 2 · para 34 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.45 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.90.30

  • Related revenue. When inventories are sold, the carrying amount of those inventories shall be recognised as an expense in the period in which the related revenue is recognised. Inventories are derecognised when they are sold; at that point they are recognised as an expense in the income statement, in the same period in which the revenue from their sale is recognised. IAS 2 · para 34 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.42 DTT A11 2022 · 3.4
  • Performance obligation. Revenue is recognised only when an entity satisfies a performance obligation by transferring a promised good or service (that is, an asset) to a customer; an asset is transferred when (or as) the customer obtains control of that asset. Therefore inventory is derecognised only when control of the asset is transferred to the customer. PwC MOA 2020 · 25.45 IFRS 15 · para 31
  • Control of an asset. Control of an asset refers to the ability to direct the use of, and obtain substantially all of the remaining benefits from, the asset; it includes the ability to prevent other entities from directing the use of, and obtaining the benefits from, the asset. The benefits of an asset are the potential cash flows (inflows or savings in outflows) that can be obtained, directly or indirectly, in many ways — for example, by
    • (a) Using the asset to produce goods or provide services (including public services);
    • (b) Using the asset to enhance the value of other assets;
    • (c) Using the asset to settle liabilities or reduce expenses;
    • (d) Selling or exchanging the asset;
    • (e) Pledging the asset to secure a loan; and
    • (f) Holding the asset. Indicators that control has passed, and shipping terms: Chapter 2 · 2.2
    PwC MOA 2020 · 25.45 IFRS 15 · para 33
  • Timing. The carrying amount of inventories is recognised as an expense when the inventories are sold, unless they form part of the cost of another asset (5.4); derecognition therefore depends on the timing of revenue recognition.

    are not the entity’s inventory at the reporting date; before goods are loaded onto the ship in a free on board (FOB) arrangement they remain the seller’s inventory (Chapter 2 · 2.2). Sales with a right of return, and the asset for the right to recover returned goods, are dealt with under the revenue requirements. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.90.30 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.90.40 IAS 2 · para 34

Some complex transactions, such as consignment sales or sale and repurchase transactions, require careful consideration as to whether inventory should be derecognised as a result of the transaction. A product delivered to another party that is held on consignment does not give rise to revenue on delivery, and so the manufacturer does not derecognise the inventory on delivery; items controlled by the entity that are held on consignment at another entity’s premises remain the consignor’s inventory. 5.2 Write-downs to net realisable value and losses of inventoriestop

The amount of any write-down of inventories to NRV and all losses of inventories are recognised as an expense in the period the write-down or loss occurs. Write-downs and losses are expensed when they occur; inventory with no future economic value is derecognised; lost or damaged consignment stock is also derecognised; measuring the write-down cross-refers to Chapter 4; and presentation and disclosure sit in cost of sales and Chapter 6. IAS 2 · para 34

  • Write-down to NRV. The amount of any write-down of inventories to net realisable value and all losses of inventories shall be recognised as an expense in the period the write-down or loss occurs. Although the inventory is not derecognised, a write-down to NRV results in the amount of the inventory that has been written down being recognised as an expense in the period in which the write-down occurs; when the NRV of an item is less than its cost, the excess is written off immediately in profit or loss. IAS 2 · para 34 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.42 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.335.10 DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.2 DTT A11 2022 · 3.4
  • No future economic value. An entity also derecognises inventory when it has no future economic value — for example, obsolete inventory. PwC MOA 2020 · 25.42 IAS 2 · para 34
  • Lost or damaged. In the consignment arrangement in Illustration 2.3.1, Manufacturer derecognises the products that are lost or damaged while in Retailer’s possession. PwC MOA 2020 · FAQ 25.46.1 IAS 2 · para 34
  • Measuring the write-down. Circumstances in which the cost of inventories may not be recoverable (4.3); item by item or groups of similar or related items, and write-downs calculated by formula (4.4); estimates of NRV (4.5). In Illustration 4.7.1, Company P writes down its inventory from a carrying amount of 100 to its NRV of 95 on 31 December 2018; the write-down is recognised as an expense in that period. IAS 2 · para 28 IAS 2 · para 29 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.335.30 IAS 2 · para 34
  • Presentation and disclosure. Write-downs to NRV are presented in cost of sales in KPMG’s view (5.3); the amount of any write-down of inventories recognised as an expense in the period is disclosed (Chapter 6 · 6.4). 5.3 Reversals of write-downstop

The amount of any reversal of any write-down of inventories, arising from an increase in NRV, is recognised as a reduction in the amount of inventories recognised as an expense in the period in which the reversal occurs. A reversal reduces the inventory expense of the period, is limited by the original write-down, is presented in cost of sales in firm views, and is disclosed as a reversal. IAS 2 · para 34

  • Reduction of the expense. The amount of any reversal of any write-down of inventories, arising from an increase in net realisable value, shall be recognised as a reduction in the amount of inventories recognised as an expense in the period in which the reversal occurs. If and when a write-down is reversed, the reversal is recognised in the income statement in the period in which the reversal occurs and the amount of inventories is increased accordingly; the reversal is offset against the amount of inventories recognised as an expense in the period. IAS 2 · para 34 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.42 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.335.20
  • Original write-down. A new assessment of NRV is made in each subsequent period; when the circumstances that caused the write-down no longer exist, or there is clear evidence of an increase in NRV because of changed economic circumstances, the write-down is reversed so that the new carrying amount is the lower of cost and the revised NRV — the reversal is limited to the amount of the original write-down (Chapter 4 · 4.7). In Illustration 4.7.1, Company P’s inventory is still on hand in 2019 and its NRV increases to 103: P recognises a reversal of 5, so that the new carrying amount is the lower of its cost of 100 and its revised NRV of 103. IAS 2 · para 33 IAS 2 · para IN15 DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.3 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.335.20 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.335.40

A gain on the sale of inventory previously written down is viewed as evidence of an increase in NRV, triggering this disclosure requirement in the period of sale. Facts

  • Entity C’s management determines that a provision of C12,000 against inventories recorded in previous years should be reversed because of changes in market conditions.
  • The tax rate is 30%. Tax relief is granted in respect of provisions recognised against specific items of inventory.
  • How should the reversal of the provision be presented in the financial statements?

Requirement

  • A reversal of a write-down arising from an increase in NRV is recognised as a reduction in the amount of inventories recognised as an expense in the period in which the reversal occurs (para 34).
  • The reversal is offset against the amount of inventories recognised as an expense in the period, and the amount of inventories is increased accordingly.

Conclusion

  • The reversal of the provision is included in cost of sales, as this was the line in the income statement against which the original provision had been charged.
  • The reversal increases the entity’s tax charge for the year by C3,600 (12,000 × 30%), reversing the tax relief received in prior periods; the adjustment to the tax charge is included in the tax line item in the income statement.

5.4 Inventories allocated to other assetstop

Some inventories are allocated to other asset accounts such as self-constructed PPE, enter the cost of construction when used in building for own use, and are then recognised as expense through depreciation of that asset.

  • Allocated to other asset accounts Some inventories may be allocated to other asset accounts, for example, inventory used as a component of self-constructed property, plant or equipment. Inventories allocated to another asset in this way are recognised as an expense during the useful life of that asset. The carrying amount of inventories is recognised as an expense when the inventories are sold unless they form part of the cost of another asset — for example, property, plant and equipment under construction. IAS 2 · para 35 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.90.30
  • Cost of construction. Inventories could be allocated to other assets — for example, inventory that is used in constructing a property for the entity’s own use. In such cases the inventory is derecognised when incorporated in the cost of construction of the other asset and recognised as part of the cost of that other asset. PwC MOA 2020 · 25.43 IAS 2 · para 35
  • Depreciation. If inventories are used by the entity rather than being sold, their cost may be capitalised as part of the cost of another asset (for example, property, plant and equipment); their cost is then recognised as an expense through depreciation of that asset. DTT A11 2022 · 3.4

Key terms

  • Recognition as an expense — the carrying amount of inventories sold is recognised as an expense in the period in which the related revenue is recognised; write-downs to NRV and all losses in the period they occur; reversals as a reduction of that expense (para 34)
  • Derecognition of inventories — when control of the asset is transferred to the customer (IFRS 15 para 31), when incorporated in the cost of another asset, or when the inventory has no future economic value
  • Amount of inventories recognised as an expense — generally the carrying amount of inventories sold, plus write-downs to NRV and losses, less reversals of write-downs; often referred to as cost of sales (paras 34, 38)
  • Write-down to net realisable value — recognised as an expense in the period it occurs; the inventory is not derecognised (para 34)
  • Reversal of a write-down — recognised as a reduction in the amount of inventories recognised as an expense in the period it occurs; limited to the amount of the original write-down (paras 33–34)
  • Inventories allocated to other assets — recognised as part of the cost of that asset, and as an expense during its useful life (para 35)
