1.0 Objectivetop

IAS 2 prescribes the accounting treatment for inventories; inventories within its scope are measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value. The Standard prescribes the accounting treatment for inventories and applies to inventories generally rather than only under a historical-cost system. The scope paragraphs separate inventories that are entirely outside the Standard from inventories that are excluded only from its measurement requirements. IAS 2 · para 1 IAS 2 · para 2 IAS 2 · para 3 IAS 2 · para 9 IAS 2 · para IN6

  • Objective. The objective of this Standard is to prescribe the accounting treatment for inventories. A primary issue in accounting for inventories is the amount of cost to be recognised as an asset and carried forward until the related revenues are recognised. This Standard provides guidance on the determination of cost and its subsequent recognition as an expense, including any write-down to net realisable value. It also provides guidance on the cost formulas that are used to assign costs to inventories. The cost formulas covered are
    • Specific identification (for items not ordinarily interchangeable)
    • FIFO
    • Weighted average cost

1.1 Definition of inventoriestop

Inventories are assets in one of three classes. The definition covers three inventory classes, what inventories encompass, intangible assets produced for resale, property held for sale versus investment property, why existing PPE is not reclassified on a decision to sell, when spare parts, stand-by equipment and servicing equipment are inventory rather than PPE, re-usable and returnable packaging used over more than one period, research-phase supplies before commercial production, and production stripping costs under IFRIC 20. IAS 2 · para 6

  • Three classes Inventories are assets:
    • Held for sale in the ordinary course of business
    • In the process of production for such sale
    • In the form of materials or supplies to be consumed in the production process or in the rendering of services
    IAS 2 · para 6 DTT A11 2022 · 2.1 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.9 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.10
  • What inventories encompass. Inventories encompass:
    • Goods purchased and held for resale (for example merchandise purchased by a retailer, or land and other property held for resale)
    • Finished goods produced
    • Work in progress being produced
    • Materials and supplies awaiting use in the production process
    Items bought or constructed with the intention of resale in the ordinary course are inventories even where they would have met the definition of property, plant and equipment (PPE) — for example buildings — had they been held for use, for rental or for administrative purposes. IAS 2 · para 8 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.11 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.14 EY IGAAP 2026 · Ch23 · section 2.3 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.20
  • Intangible assets. Inventory may include intangible assets that are being produced for resale — for example software. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.10 IAS 38 · para 2–3
  • Property held for sale versus investment property.
    • Classifying property as held for sale in the ordinary course of business (inventory) or as held for capital appreciation (investment property) can be complex and may require judgement; the entity’s business model plays a key role on initial recognition.
    • Property is subsequently reclassified only on an actual change in use.

Illustration: 1.1.1 — Pipeline fill classified as inventoriesDTT A11 2022 · 2.1-2 IAS 2 · para 6 IAS 2 · para 9 IAS 16 · para 6

Facts

  • Company A operates a crude-oil pipeline for a usage fee; it does not produce or distribute oil. Buyer and seller negotiate the sales price independently; either pays A for transport.
  • The pipeline must stay full to be operational. During initial construction A purchases oil to fill the line.
  • Once operational, A charges a fixed fee for its transportation services and in effect swaps crude oil pushed in by a seller for crude oil of the same grade and quality delivered to the customer at the exit point.
  • A bears the risk of loss from theft or line loss above the contractual maximums; such losses are rare and normally arise from a pipeline spill covered by insurance.

Classification

  • The fill meets the definition of an asset and is recognised at cost when acquired.
  • It does not meet the definition of PPE under IAS 16.
  • It is classified as inventories because it is held in the form of materials or supplies to be consumed in the production process or in the rendering of services.

Measurement

  • No accounting transaction takes place at each swap of crude oil, so no step-up in the value of inventories is recognised.
  • The fill is measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value throughout the term of the pipeline’s operations.

Pipeline fill — from construction to operation

Initial construction A purchases oil to fill the line — an asset recognised at cost, classified as inventories (materials or supplies consumed in rendering services)
Operation A charges a fixed fee and in effect swaps crude oil pushed in by a seller for crude oil of the same grade and quality delivered at the exit point
Each swap No accounting transaction takes place — no step-up in the value of inventories Throughout the pipeline’s operations — lower of cost and net realisable value (para 9)

Illustration: 1.1.4 — Re-usable bottles classified as property, plant and equipmentKPMG Insights 2019/20 · Example 2 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.40 IAS 16 · para 6

Facts

  • Company C produces and distributes bottled drinks and receives a deposit for the bottles when drinks are sold.
  • C is required to buy back empty bottles, which are used again in future periods.
  • The bottles are used over more than one period and are not bought for the purpose of resale.

Not inventories

  • Packaging sold to customers but returned to be re-used is not inventory when used over more than one period.
  • The bottles are not held for resale in the ordinary course of business.

Property, plant and equipment

  • The bottles are an item of PPE — the same more-than-one-period test as for spare parts (Illustration 1.1.3).
  • The refundable deposits received from customers are accounted for separately (KPMG Insights 3.12.370).

Illustration: 1.1.5 — Assets acquired for sale at the end of a lease termDTT A11 2022 · 2.1-1 IAS 2 · para 6

Facts

  • Lessor X. Leases assets ordinarily under three-year agreements; at lease end the lessee may return or acquire the asset.
  • Extension option. Some leases give the lessee an additional three months to return or acquire the asset; the option must be exercised before the main lease term ends.
  • Residual value guarantor A. Under residual value guarantee contracts with X, A will purchase the assets from X at the end of each lease term at a predetermined price, and receives a fee for the guarantee.
  • When the extension option is exercised. Ownership transfers to A at the end of the main lease term for the predetermined price; during the extension X passes the rental income to A; at the end of the extension A sells the asset in the market or to the lessee.
  • Incidental rentals. Rental income A receives in the extension period is incidental to A’s principal activities — providing residual value guarantee contracts and selling the assets acquired.

Inventories

  • For the period from acquisition at the end of the main lease term to the date of sale, A must establish how the assets are used in the business — inventories or PPE.
  • A acquires the assets to profit from selling them in the market → classified as inventories: assets held for sale in the ordinary course of business.

Not property, plant and equipment

  • The assets are not PPE under IAS 16.
  • They are not held primarily for rental by A to others.
  • They are not expected to be used during more than one period.

Illustration: 1.1.6 — Property leased out for a short period before sale remains inventoryDTT A11 2022 · 2.1-4 IAS 2 · para 6 IAS 40 · para 57(d)

Facts

  • Entity A purchases and develops residential properties solely for sale in the ordinary course of business.
  • The properties are classified as inventory until sold — generally shortly after development is completed.
  • In some cases, shortly before sale, A leases out the property (or a portion of it) on a temporary basis — for example to optimise net cash flows until a buyer is found, or to enhance the value of the property by incorporating tenants.
  • The lease agreements are not entered into with the objective of generating rental earnings or capital appreciation in the long term.
  • Rental earnings before the sale are not significant compared with the sale price of the property.

Change in use (IAS 40 para 57(d))

  • Inception of an operating lease is cited as an example of evidence of a change in use in IAS 40 para 57(d).
  • That evidence does not automatically trigger reclassification from inventory to investment property.
  • Reclassification also requires the property to meet the definition of investment property — management’s intention must have changed so that the property is now held to earn rentals, for capital appreciation, or both.

Conclusion

  • The lease period before sale is limited and rental earnings are not significant compared with the expected sale price → A’s intention, strategy and business model for sale in the ordinary course have not changed.
  • A is not permitted to reclassify the property from inventory to investment property under IAS 40 para 57(d).
  • The property remains classified as inventory.

Lease before sale — IAS 40 para 57(d)

1.3 Inventories excluded from the measurement requirements onlytop

IAS 2 does not apply to the measurement of inventories held by industry-practice producers measuring at net realisable value or by commodity broker-traders measuring at fair value less costs to sell; the other requirements of IAS 2 — including presentation and disclosure — still apply. The carve-outs open with inventories entirely outside the Standard versus those excluded from measurement only, then producers measuring at NRV, producers that elect to carry at cost, commodity broker-traders measuring at fair value less costs to sell, how to judge whether an entity acts as a broker-trader including “near future”, and the Basis for Conclusions on those carve-outs. Illustration: 1.3.2 — Emission rights and green certificatesEY IGAAP 2026 · Ch23 · section 2.3.1.C EY IGAAP 2026 · Ch23 · section 2.3.1.D KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.73 IAS 2 · para 3(b)

Schemes

  • Emission rights. Under cap-and-trade schemes an entity must deliver emissions certificates to a third party (for example a regulator) to emit pollutants legally; the government grants certificates for a compliance period, and entities trade certificates to match their emissions. Rights may be granted or purchased.
  • Green certificates. Awarded on green energy already produced — unlike emission rights, which reflect a future emissions limit.
  • No specific IFRS guidance. In KPMG’s view a participant chooses an accounting policy, applied consistently — intangible assets, or inventories (an input consumed in the production process) — whether the allowances are bought or received from the government.

Classification

  • Inventories where held for sale in the ordinary course of business, or to settle an emissions liability in the ordinary course.
  • Own use. Entities using rights for their own purposes might account for them as intangible assets — at cost, at revalued amount, or under a ‘net liability’ approach (IAS 38).
  • Integrated entity. An entity holding emission rights both for own use and for trading accounts for those holdings separately.

Measurement

  • Purchased emission rights and green certificates recognised as inventories are measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value, unless held by commodity broker-traders.
  • Commodity broker-traders account for them as inventories and may measure at the lower of cost and net realisable value or at fair value less costs to sell, as permitted by para 3(b).
  • Where measured at fair value less costs to sell, the IAS 2 disclosure requirements continue to apply.

Illustration: 1.3.3 — Net realisable value is entity-specific; fair value is notPwC MOA 2020 · FAQ 25.8.1 IAS 2 · para 6 IAS 2 · para 7 IFRS 13 EY IGAAP 2026 · Ch23 · section 3.3

Facts

  • An entity holds mineral inventories.
  • The current market price is C10 per tonne.
  • The entity is in a forward contract to sell the inventories at C12 per tonne.

Measurement bases

  • Net realisable value is the net amount the entity expects to realise from sale in the ordinary course of business — an entity-specific value.
  • Fair value reflects the price at which an orderly transaction to sell the same inventory would take place between market participants in the principal (or most advantageous) market — not entity-specific; measured under IFRS 13.
  • Net realisable value may not equal fair value less costs to sell. Fair value suits broker-traders, which have access to ready markets; net realisable value suits producers, which might not.

Conclusion

  • Fair value is C10 per tonne; net realisable value is C12 per tonne.

Advertising and promotional items — samples and catalogues

Samples and catalogues handed out free of charge for advertising and promotional purposes are not inventories; their cost is recognised as an expense. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.50 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.60 IAS 38 · para 69–70

Manufactured samplesSome entities manufacture samples they intend to hand out free of charge for advertising and promotional purposes (for example beauty products). 1.5 Scope outcomestop

Within IAS 2, including its measurement requirements.Inventories are measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value: Chapter 2 (when an item is recognised as inventory), Chapter 3 (cost) and Chapter 4 (net realisable value and write-downs). IAS 2 · para 9
Excluded from the scope of IAS 2.Financial instruments and biological assets / agricultural produce at the point of harvest are accounted for under IAS 32 and IFRS 9, or IAS 41; no IAS 2 requirement applies. IAS 2 · para 2
Excluded from the measurement requirements only.Paragraph 3 inventories are measured at net realisable value or at fair value less costs to sell, with changes in profit or loss; presentation and disclosure requirements still apply, including the carrying amount of inventories carried at fair value less costs to sell (Chapter 6 · 6.3). Key terms

  • Inventories — assets held for sale in the ordinary course of business, in the process of production for such sale, or in the form of materials or supplies to be consumed in production or in rendering services (para 6)
  • Scope exclusion — financial instruments; biological assets related to agricultural activity and agricultural produce at the point of harvest (para 2)
  • Excluded from the measurement requirements only — industry-practice producers at net realisable value and commodity broker-traders at fair value less costs to sell; other requirements still apply (paras 3–5)
  • Commodity broker-trader — buys or sells commodities for others or on its own account (para 5)
  • Net realisable value — entity-specific; may not equal fair value less costs to sell (para 7)
  • Cost at the point of harvest — fair value less costs to sell under IAS 41 becomes cost for IAS 2 (para 20)
