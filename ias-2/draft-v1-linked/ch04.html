4.0 Measurement of inventoriestop

Inventories are measured at cost on initial recognition and, after initial recognition, at the lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV). Subsequent measurement opens with the lower of cost and NRV, recalls how cost is determined, charges irrecoverable cost as an expense, and writes the excess of cost over NRV down immediately. Costs necessary to make the sale

4.2 Net realisable value and fair valuetop

NRV for inventories may not equal fair value less costs to sell. NRV is entity-specific while fair value is market-based; favourable binding sales contracts can make NRV differ from fair value less costs to sell; and broker-traders tend to use fair values while producers tend to use NRV. IAS 2 · para 7

  • Entity-specific value. Net realisable value refers to the net amount that an entity expects to realise from the sale of inventory in the ordinary course of business. Fair value reflects the price at which an orderly transaction to sell the same inventory in the principal (or most advantageous) market for that inventory would take place between market participants at the measurement date. The former is an entity-specific value; the latter is not. Net realisable value for inventories may not equal fair value less costs to sell. Fair value is the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date (IFRS 13). That price is a market-participant price. IAS 2 · para 7 IAS 2 · para 6 DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.1 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.330.10
  • Favourable binding sales contracts. NRV differs from fair value less costs to sell. NRV will not equal fair value less costs to sell, for example, when the reporting entity has secured favourable binding sales contracts that have not been affected by more recent adverse market conditions. DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.1
  • Broker-traders and producers. Fair values are the appropriate measurement bases for broker-traders, because they have access to ready markets; NRV is appropriate for producers, because they might not have such access. Where inventories are measured at fair value, that fair value is measured in accordance with IFRS 13. For mineral inventories with a current market price of C10 per tonne and a forward contract to sell at C12 per tonne, fair value is C10 per tonne but NRV is C12 per tonne (Illustration 1.3.3). PwC MOA 2020 · FAQ 25.8.1 IAS 2 · para 3(b)

4.3 Circumstances in which the cost of inventories may not be recoverabletop

The cost of inventories may not be recoverable if they are damaged, wholly or partially obsolete or their selling prices have declined, or if the estimated costs of completion or of making the sale have increased; the inventories are then written down below cost to NRV. Cost may not be recoverable when inventories are damaged, obsolete or their selling prices have fallen, or when completion or selling costs have risen; the principal situations where NRV is below cost are listed; and exchange-rate movements can also force an NRV write-down. IAS 2 · para 28

  • Sale or use. The cost of inventories may not be recoverable if those inventories are damaged, if they have become wholly or partially obsolete, or if their selling prices have declined. The cost of inventories may also not be recoverable if the estimated costs of completion or the estimated costs to be incurred to make the sale have increased. The practice of writing inventories down below cost to net realisable value is consistent with the view that assets should not be carried in excess of amounts expected to be realised from their sale or use. 4.4 Item by item and groups of similar or related itemstop

Inventories are usually written down to NRV item by item; in some circumstances it may be appropriate to group similar or related items, but not a classification of inventory or all the inventories in an operating segment. Write-downs are usually made item by item; clothing textiles may need a grouped NRV test for a season; and retailers generally do not write down a whole department store at once. IAS 2 · para 29

  • Item by item. Inventories are usually written down to net realisable value item by item. In some circumstances, however, it may be appropriate to group similar or related items. This may be the case with items of inventory relating to the same product line that have similar purposes or end uses, are produced and marketed in the same geographical area, and cannot be practicably evaluated separately from other items in that product line. It is not appropriate to write inventories down on the basis of a classification of inventory, for example, finished goods, or all the inventories in a particular operating segment. IAS 2 · para 29 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.36 DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.1 DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.2 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.340.10 EY IGAAP 2026 · Ch23 · section 3.3
  • Clothing textiles. In the clothing textile industry it may not be possible to determine selling prices for each textile individually, and therefore it may be necessary to perform the NRV assessment on all textiles that will be used to produce clothing for a particular season. NRV assessed for all textiles for a season. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.340.20
  • Retailers. Retailers do not generally determine NRV write-downs on the basis of the whole department store, because different departments usually have different margins. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.340.30

4.5 Estimates of net realisable valuetop

Estimates of NRV are based on the most reliable evidence available at the time the estimates are made, and take into consideration the purpose for which the inventory is held. IAS 2 · para 30 IAS 2 · para 31

  • Most reliable evidence. Estimates of net realisable value are based on the most reliable evidence available at the time the estimates are made, of the amount the inventories are expected to realise. These estimates take into consideration fluctuations of price or cost directly relating to events occurring after the end of the period to the extent that such events confirm conditions existing at the end of the period. When estimating NRV, management considers all of the facts relating to the inventories and the operating environment at the time the estimates are made. IAS 2 · para 30 DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.1 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.39 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.360.10
  • Purpose. Estimates of net realisable value also take into consideration the purpose for which the inventory is held. For example, the net realisable value of the quantity of inventory held to satisfy firm sales or service contracts is based on the contract price. If the sales contracts are for less than the inventory quantities held, the net realisable value of the excess is based on general selling prices. Provisions may arise from firm sales contracts in excess of inventory quantities held or from firm purchase contracts. Such provisions are dealt with under IAS 37 Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets. IAS 2 · para 31 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.38 DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.1 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.350.10 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.390.10

Events after the reporting period

Events after the reporting date that show cost above NRV at that date require a write-down at the reporting date; IAS 10 treats post-balance-sheet inventory sales as adjusting evidence of NRV.

  • Reporting date. Events after the reporting date that evidence cost above NRV at the reporting date are written down to NRV at that date. Events after the reporting date may provide evidence that the cost of inventory exceeds its NRV at the reporting date; in these cases, the inventory is written down to its NRV at the reporting date. Events occurring between the balance sheet date and the date of completion of the financial statements — for example, a subsequent reduction in selling prices — are considered in arriving at NRV at the balance sheet date, to the extent that they confirm conditions existing at the end of the year. Purpose for which the inventory is held — intended use

Estimated selling price follows the intended use of the inventory; operating losses alone do not trigger an automatic write-down.

  • Intended use. The estimated selling price takes into account the intended use of the items. The calculation of NRV takes account of the intended use of the inventory — for example, the NRV of inventories held to satisfy a particular sales or service contract is based on the contract price (firm sales contracts). Firm sales contracts

Firm sales contracts set the NRV selling price for inventory held to satisfy them; sales-commitment derivatives in IFRS 9 interact with that measurement; inventory is written down when the cost of fulfilling a sales contract exceeds the contract price; and onerous future purchase or sale contracts may need provisions.

  • Firm sales contracts. The determination of NRV takes into account firm sales contracts that have been entered into.
    • The NRV of inventory held to satisfy firm sales contracts is based on the selling price under those contracts
    • The NRV of inventory held in excess of firm sales contracts is based on general selling prices without regard to the firm sales contracts
    KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.390.10 IAS 2 · para 31 DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.1 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.38
  • Sales commitment derivatives. Where forward contracts entered into to manage inventory levels are in the scope of IFRS 9 and accounted for under its guidance, in KPMG’s view the NRV of the inventory held to satisfy firm sales contracts is based on the contract price including the fair value of the related sales commitment derivatives; the NRV of inventory held in excess of firm sales contracts is based on general selling prices without regard to the specific sales contracts (Illustration 4.5.10). KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.390.50 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.390.60
  • Cost of fulfilling. If an entity has a contract to sell inventory for less than the cost of fulfilling the obligations under the contract, the inventory held to satisfy that contract is written down by the amount by which the cost of fulfilling the obligations exceeds the selling price. If that excess is greater than the carrying amount of the inventory, the entity considers the guidance in IAS 37 on onerous contracts. KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.390.70 IAS 2 · para 31
  • Onerous contracts. Contracts for future sales, or for future purchases of raw materials, might give rise to provisions where such contracts are onerous — for example, a future sales contract set at a price below the entity’s cost of production of the relevant goods, or a purchase commitment for goods or raw materials at a price above the price at which the entity can sell on the goods or manufacture the finished product from the raw materials. Onerous contracts are accounted for under IAS 37. PwC MOA 2020 · 25.38 IAS 2 · para 31

4.6 Materials and other supplies held for use in productiontop

Materials held for production are not written down if the finished products will realise at least cost; NRV then follows the finished products’ estimated selling price unless the materials will be sold in their current form; excess materials that will be sold are measured on their own anticipated sale; lower replacement cost does not by itself force a write-down; and parts and sub-assemblies may need review when finished goods are written down.

  • Finished products. Written down only when the cost of the finished products exceeds NRV. Materials and other supplies held for use in the production of inventories are not written down below cost if the finished products in which they will be incorporated are expected to be sold at or above cost. However, when a decline in the price of materials indicates that the cost of the finished products exceeds net realisable value, the materials are written down to net realisable value. In such circumstances, the replacement cost of the materials may be the best available measure of their net realisable value. A write-down is not necessary where the NRV of material inventories is less than the purchase price, provided that the finished goods into which the materials are to be incorporated can still be sold at a profit after incorporating the materials at cost price. 4.7 Reversal of write-downstop

A new assessment of NRV is made in each subsequent period; a write-down is reversed when the circumstances that caused it no longer exist or there is clear evidence of an increase in NRV — limited to the amount of the original write-down. A write-down is reversed when a new assessment shows NRV has recovered, in whole or in part, with the reversal recognised in profit or loss, while items still on hand after a long period may still raise obsolescence questions. IAS 2 · para 33

  • New assessment. A new assessment is made of net realisable value in each subsequent period. When the circumstances that previously caused inventories to be written down below cost no longer exist or when there is clear evidence of an increase in net realisable value because of changed economic circumstances, the amount of the write-down is reversed (ie the reversal is limited to the amount of the original write-down) so that the new carrying amount is the lower of the cost and the revised net realisable value. This occurs, for example, when an item of inventory that is carried at net realisable value, because its selling price has declined, is still on hand in a subsequent period and its selling price has increased. The Standard describes the circumstances that would trigger a reversal of a write-down of inventories recognised in a prior period. IAS 2 · para 33 IAS 2 · para IN15 DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.3 PwC MOA 2020 · 25.41 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.335.20 EY IGAAP 2026 · Ch23 · section 3.3.1.C
  • All or part. If the circumstances that caused the write-down cease to exist, such that all or part of the write-down is no longer needed, it is reversed to that extent. PwC MOA 2020 · 25.41
  • Profit or loss. The amount of the write-down is reversed through profit or loss so that the new carrying amount is the lower of the cost and the revised NRV. Reversals are recognised in profit or loss in the period in which the reversal occurs, as a reduction in the amount of inventories recognised as an expense in the period (Chapter 5 · 5.3). DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.3 KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 3.8.335.20 IAS 2 · para 34
  • Obsolescence. When an item carried at NRV because its selling price had declined is still on hand in a subsequent period and its selling price has increased, it would still be necessary, if the item had been on hand for a long time, to consider whether there might be obsolescence issues. DTT A11 2022 · 3.3.3

Accounting policy disclosed

  • Building-materials group (CRH plc 2022) states inventories at the lower of cost and NRV.
  • Cost uses FIFO, with weighted average where appropriate — same-nature / same-use consistency.
  • Cost includes
    • Expenditure to acquire and bring inventories to present location and condition
    • Finished goods and work in progress include direct materials, direct labour and attributable overheads on normal operating capacity
    • Borrowing costs excluded
  • NRV = estimated proceeds of sale less costs to complete and costs of marketing, selling and distribution.

Net realisable value

  • Estimates consider price fluctuations after the period end only to the extent they confirm period-end conditions (para 30).
  • Also weigh buyer preferences, obsolescence and perishability when assessing recoverability.
  • Materials for production are not written down below cost if the finished goods they enter are expected to be sold at or above cost (para 32).
  • Climate-related and policy risks are flagged as a future valuation consideration; short inventory turnover meant no material climate-related write-downs in that year.

Conclusion

  • The policy brings together the lower of cost and NRV, the cost formula, what is included in cost, how NRV is estimated and the materials rule.
  • Write-downs are reassessed in each subsequent period and reversed in the circumstances in paragraph 33 (4.7).

Key terms

  • Lower of cost and net realisable value — the measurement of inventories after initial recognition at cost (para 9)
  • Net realisable value — the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business less the estimated costs of completion and the estimated costs necessary to make the sale; an entity-specific value (paras 6–7)
  • Fair value — the price that would be received to sell an asset in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date; not an entity-specific value (paras 6–7)
  • Costs necessary to make the sale — not limited to incremental costs (June 2021 IFRIC Update)
  • Item by item — the usual basis for writing inventories down; similar or related items may be grouped in some circumstances (para 29)
  • Firm sales contracts — NRV of the quantity held to satisfy them is based on the contract price; the excess on general selling prices (para 31)
  • Replacement cost — may be the best available measure of the NRV of materials (para 32)
  • Reversal of a write-down — limited to the amount of the original write-down, so that the new carrying amount is the lower of cost and revised NRV (para 33)
