4.0Scope of this chaptertop

Chapter 4 defines five elements — assets, liabilities and equity for financial position, and income and expenses for financial performance. The elements are linked to the economic resources, claims and changes in them discussed in Chapter 1 (Table 4.1). Classification then sorts assets, liabilities, equity, income or expenses on the basis of shared characteristics — such as nature, role (or function) in the business activities, and measurement — for presentation and disclosure purposes. 4.1Structure of the elements — Assets − Liabilities = Equitytop

Assets and liabilities are defined first; equity is the residual; income and expenses are the changes in assets and liabilities that change equity, other than transactions with holders of equity claims. The Framework follows an asset–liability model: the definitions of income and expenses rest on changes in assets and liabilities. The Board found it more effective, efficient and rigorous to define assets and liabilities first than to define income and expenses first and treat assets and liabilities as by-products of a matching process. CF 2018 · para 4.2CF 2018 · para 4.63CF 2018 · para 4.68–4.69CF 2018 · para BC4.94PwC MOA 2020 · 1.63

Performance derived from the movement in financial position — a “balance sheet approach”. EY describes the approach to the elements as what is sometimes called a ‘balance sheet approach’ to recording financial performance: performance for a period is essentially derived as part of the overall movement in the entity’s financial position, so all non-owner changes in equity comprise performance in its broadest sense. EY notes the difficulty this created for the Board — the well-established idea of profit contains only a subset of that performance (see 7.5). EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7

4.1.1Asset ∥ Liability — parallel definitions, different linkerstop

Asset and liability share one mirrored structure: a present right or obligation, a potential for benefits in or a transfer out, and past events. Probability is not part of either existence test — low probability can still meet the definition, and recognition and measurement absorb much of the uncertainty. CF 2018 · para 4.3–4.5CF 2018 · para 4.14–4.15CF 2018 · para 4.26–4.27CF 2018 · para 4.37–4.38CF 2018 · para 5.15EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2.2EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.3.2 The separate definition of an economic resource and the use of “present” and “as a result of past events” in both definitions show the parallel.

  • A separate definition of an economic resource shows the parallel. Moving the references to flows of economic benefits into the supporting definition of an economic resource emphasises that an asset (or liability) is the resource (or obligation) itself — not the ultimate inflow (or outflow) it may produce — and streamlines the two definitions. CF 2018 · para BC4.6–BC4.7CF 2018 · para 4.4EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7
  • “Present” and “as a result of past events” in both definitions. Identifying the past event tells an entity how to report it — for example, how to classify and present the resulting income, expenses or cash flows. A past event alone does not confirm that the asset or liability still exists, so “present” was kept in the liability definition and added to the asset definition, emphasising the parallel. The linkers are deliberately asymmetric, but both ask what the entity can actually do. Control asks which entity obtains the benefits from a present right (and can prevent others). No practical ability to avoid asks whether a duty binds the entity in substance — even when a theoretical exit still exists. The Board chose “no practical ability to avoid” because it conveys what an entity is obliged to do rather than the probable outcome, and because it mirrors “practical ability” used in some Standards to assess control of an asset and “present ability” in the control guidance. 4.2Asset — right, potential, controltop

An asset is a present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events. An economic resource is a right that has the potential to produce economic benefits. The three aspects — right, potential, control — explain the definition; they are not a sequence to tick before moving to Liability. CF 2018 · para 4.3–4.5PwC MOA 2020 · 1.64EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2 The asset definition makes clear that the asset is the right rather than the benefits, that the benefits are economic rather than social or environmental, and that a right with a low probability of benefits can still be an asset.

  • The asset is the right, not the benefits. Although an asset derives its value from its potential to produce future benefits, what the entity controls is the present right that contains that potential; it does not control the future benefits. “Economic resource” covers all resources with that potential, not only those with a market, and emphasises rights over a physical object rather than the object. CF 2018 · para BC4.25–BC4.26CF 2018 · para 4.17EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2.2
  • Economic benefits — not social or environmental benefits. Because the Board currently focuses on for-profit entities, the asset definition stays on resources with the potential to produce economic benefits, and the liability definition on obligations to transfer an economic resource. CF 2018 · para BC4.27
  • Low probability can still be an asset. A right can be an asset even if the probability that it will produce economic benefits is low — that low probability affects what information is provided, whether the asset is recognised and how it is measured. CF 2018 · para 4.14–4.15CF 2018 · para 5.15–5.17DTT A2 2022 · 7.1EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2.2EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 8.2.1

Right — rights take many forms and need not arise from contract. CF 2018 · para 4.6–4.13PwC MOA 2020 · 1.65EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2.1 Some rights correspond to another party’s obligation and others do not, rights come from several sources, and goods or services consumed immediately are rights that exist only momentarily. Not every right is an asset of the entity, an entity has no right to benefits from itself, a physical object is a set of rights, how far a bundle of rights can be split is an open question, and identifiability, separability and goodwill are outside the definition.

  • Rights that correspond to another party’s obligation — to receive cash; to receive goods or services; to exchange economic resources on favourable terms (a forward contract to buy on currently favourable terms, or an option to buy); to benefit from another party’s obligation to transfer if a specified uncertain future event occurs. CF 2018 · para 4.6(a)EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2.1
  • Rights that do not correspond to another party’s obligation — rights over physical objects such as property, plant and equipment or inventories (a right to use the object, or to benefit from the residual value of a leased object); rights to use intellectual property. CF 2018 · para 4.6(b)EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2.1
  • Sources of rights. Many rights come from contract, legislation or similar means (owning or leasing an object, owning a debt or equity instrument, a registered patent); others from know-how not in the public domain, or from another party’s constructive obligation. The term “right” covers such know-how; “other sources of value” was rejected as too vague. CF 2018 · para 4.7CF 2018 · para BC4.35–BC4.36EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2.1
  • Goods or services consumed immediately. The right to the benefits of, for example, employee services exists momentarily until consumption, when the consumption is recognised as an expense — consistent with IFRS 2 treating employee services received as an asset immediately consumed. CF 2018 · para 4.8CF 2018 · para BC4.37EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2.1
  • Not every right is an asset of the entity. It must have the potential to produce benefits beyond those available to all other parties, and be controlled. Rights available to all parties without significant cost — public rights of way, public-domain know-how — are typically not assets; this avoids compelling entities to identify a large array of rights. CF 2018 · para 4.9CF 2018 · para BC4.38–BC4.39EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2.1
  • No right to benefits from itself. Treasury shares and debt or equity instruments issued by one legal entity and held by another within the same reporting entity are not economic resources of that reporting entity. CF 2018 · para 4.10EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2.1
  • A physical object is a set of rights. In principle each right is a separate asset; related rights — to use, sell, pledge and others — are often one unit of account. Describing the set of rights as the physical object (for example, a ship as a single asset) is often the most concise faithful representation; the Board saw no advantage in two types of asset (resource vs right). Componentising rights is consistent with the right-of-use asset in IFRS 16. CF 2018 · para 4.11–4.12CF 2018 · para BC4.28–BC4.31DTT A2 2022 · 7.2.1EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.2.1
  • “Bundles of rights” — how far an asset can be split is the open question. A physical object can be “sliced and diced” for accounting purposes — in some circumstances a company would book a right to use an aircraft rather than the aircraft itself; the challenge is determining to what extent an asset can be split into different rights and the effect on recognition and derecognition. KPMG New Foundation 2018 · p2
  • Identifiability, separability and goodwill are outside the definition. Separability or a contractual/legal origin may make an asset easier to identify, measure and describe — relevant to recognition — but is not part of the definition; the Framework does not mention goodwill (core goodwill meets the asset definition per the IFRS 3 Basis for Conclusions). CF 2018 · para BC4.32–BC4.34

Illustration: 4.2.1 — Shares held as trustee — no control of the benefits PwC MOA 2020 · FAQ 1.65.1 CF 2018 · para 4.4 CF 2018 · para 4.20 CF 2018 · para 4.25

Facts

  • Entity A acts as trustee for entity B under a legal arrangement, holding listed shares on B’s behalf.
  • B makes all investment decisions; A acts on B’s instructions.
  • A earns a trustee fee; any dividends or profit (loss) on the investments belong to B.

Conclusion

  • A does not recognise the shares as its asset, even though it is in possession of them.
  • A does not control the future economic benefits — they flow to B; A earns its fee regardless of how the shares perform.
  • The shares do not meet the criteria of an asset in A’s balance sheet.

Framework

  • Control is the present ability to direct the use of a resource and obtain the benefits that may flow from it.
  • Agent custody: a resource controlled by a principal and held by an agent is not an asset of the agent.

Illustration: 4.2.2 — Start-up costs before the development phase — no asset PwC MOA 2020 · FAQ 1.66.1 CF 2018 · para 4.14 CF 2018 · para 4.18

Facts

  • Entity C incurs start-up costs but has not met the IAS 38 paragraph 57 criteria for recognising the costs of constructing an intangible asset.
  • C may expect the activity to lead to an intangible asset and, in turn, to profits.

Conclusion

  • The expenditure does not meet the definition of an asset.
  • FAQ outcome — expectation wording, not the 2018 definition test: until the development phase is entered, C does not have sufficient expectation or control of any economic benefits that might flow (see Sources differ — asset test in the PwC FAQs).

Framework

  • Potential must already exist: the right must already exist and, in at least one circumstance, produce for the entity benefits beyond those available to all other parties.
  • Expenditure is not conclusive proof: it may show that benefits were sought, but does not prove that an asset was obtained.

Illustration: 4.2.3 — Redundancy payments on relocating a factory — no controlled resource PwC MOA 2020 · FAQ 1.66.2 CF 2018 · para 4.18–4.19

Facts

  • Entity D moves a factory to Asia to streamline operations and significantly reduce future cash outflows.
  • D pays redundancy to employees at the present factory as part of the relocation.

Conclusion

  • The redundancy payments are not recognised as an asset.
  • They do not give D control over a resource, although they reduce D’s future cash outflows.
  • An asset is recognised only if the entity controls the resource.

Framework

  • Expenditure and assets do not necessarily coincide; control links a resource to the entity.

Illustration: 4.2.4 — Property granted by a government — an asset without expenditure PwC MOA 2020 · FAQ 1.66.3 CF 2018 · para 4.14 CF 2018 · para 4.18

Facts

  • Entity E receives a property from the government to develop a leisure park, as part of the government’s efforts to increase tourism.
  • The property’s fair value can be reliably measured.
  • E controls the leisure park’s development and operation.

Conclusion

  • The grant is a government grant; the property is measured at fair value (IAS 20 paragraph 23).
  • FAQ outcome — expected-flow and reliable-measurement wording, not the 2018 definition or recognition test: E controls the property, from which future economic benefits are expected to flow when the park operates; its value can be reliably measured.
  • FAQ outcome — criteria met: the definition and recognition criteria of assets are both met; the grant is presented in the balance sheet in accordance with IAS 20 (see Sources differ — asset test in the PwC FAQs).

Framework

  • No expenditure needed: rights a government grants free of charge, or that another party donates, can be assets.

4.3Liability — obligation, transfer, past eventstop

A liability is a present obligation of the entity to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events. All three criteria must be satisfied:

  • (a) An obligation;
  • (b) To transfer an economic resource;
  • (c) A present obligation that exists as a result of past events. The main changes from 2010 are the deletion of the expected outflow and the replacement of “resources embodying economic benefits” with “economic resource”. CF 2018 · para 4.26–4.27CF 2018 · para BC4.44CF 2018 · para BC4.46PwC MOA 2020 · 1.67–1.68

An obligation can meet the definition even if the probability of a transfer is low; that probability is considered in recognition, measurement and the notes.   • Judgement, guided by Standards. Obligation — a duty or responsibility the entity has no practical ability to avoid, always owed to another party. The other party may be a person, an entity, a group or society at large, and need not be identified. CF 2018 · para 4.28–4.29PwC MOA 2020 · 1.68EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.3.1 An obligation for one party is a right for another, and an obligation can be legal or constructive or conditional on the entity’s own future action. The going concern basis and significantly more adverse consequences bear on whether a transfer can be avoided, and existence uncertainty can leave it unclear whether a liability exists.

  • An obligation for one party is a right for another. That other party has a right to receive the resource — sometimes easier to identify — but particular Standards may set different recognition or measurement for the liability and the corresponding asset. The counterparty controls an asset even when the obligation is constructive or conditional; for environmental obligations the right is controlled by society at large. CF 2018 · para 4.30CF 2018 · para BC4.59–BC4.61EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.3.1
  • Legal or constructive. Many obligations are legally enforceable under contract or legislation; a constructive obligation arises from customary practices, published policies or specific statements that the entity has no practical ability to act inconsistently with.   • Conditional on the entity’s own future action — operating a business or in a market on a future date, or exercising options in a contract — is an obligation if the entity has no practical ability to avoid taking that action. CF 2018 · para 4.32EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.3.1
  • Going concern. Preparing financial statements on a going concern basis implies no practical ability to avoid a transfer that could be avoided only by liquidating or ceasing to trade. If another party seeks compensation for alleged wrongdoing, it may be uncertain whether the act occurred, whether the entity committed it or how the law applies; until resolved, it is uncertain whether a liability exists. 4.4Equity — residual after liabilitiestop

Equity is the residual interest in the assets of the entity after deducting all its liabilities. Equity claims are claims on that residual — claims against the entity that do not meet the definition of a liability. The Framework does not start from instrument labels; the liability definition comes first and what remains is equity. CF 2018 · para 4.63–4.64CF 2018 · para BC4.89PwC MOA 2020 · 1.72EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.4

4.5Income and expenses — changes in assets and liabilities, excluding holders of equity claimstop

Income and expenses are defined through changes in assets and liabilities that change equity, other than contributions from or distributions to holders of equity claims. Income is increases in assets, or decreases in liabilities, that result in increases in equity, other than those relating to contributions from holders of equity claims. Expenses are decreases in assets, or increases in liabilities, that result in decreases in equity, other than those relating to distributions to holders of equity claims. It follows that contributions from holders of equity claims are not income, and distributions to them are not expenses. CF 2018 · para 4.68–4.70PwC MOA 2020 · 1.78–1.79EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.5

4.6Unit of accounttop

The unit of account is the right or group of rights, the obligation or group of obligations, or the group of rights and obligations, to which recognition criteria and measurement concepts are applied. It is a grouping choice, not a further element. It is selected considering how recognition and measurement will apply to the item and the related income and expenses; one unit may be used for recognition and another for measurement — for example, contracts recognised individually but measured as a portfolio — and items may be aggregated or separated for presentation and disclosure. CF 2018 · para 4.48–4.49CF 2018 · para BC4.75PwC MOA 2020 · 1.69DTT A2 2022 · 7.4EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.1.1 A partial transfer can create separate units of account, and the unit is selected to give relevant information and a faithful representation at an acceptable cost. Rights and obligations from the same source may be inseparable or separable, several units of account are possible, and the unit is set in Standards without ranking the factors.

  • Partial transfers. If part of an asset or liability is transferred, the transferred and retained components may become separate units of account. CF 2018 · para 4.50CF 2018 · para 5.26–5.33EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.1.1EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 8.3
  • Selected to give useful information — relevant. Grouping rights and obligations may be more relevant if they (i) cannot be, or are unlikely to be, the subject of separate transactions; (ii) cannot or are unlikely to expire in different patterns; (iii) have similar economic characteristics and risks; or (iv) are used together to produce cash flows and are measured by reference to interdependent future cash flows. CF 2018 · para 4.51(a)EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.1.1
  • Selected to give useful information — faithful representation. It may be necessary to treat rights or obligations from different sources as one unit, or to separate those from a single source; unrelated rights and obligations may need separate recognition and measurement. CF 2018 · para 4.51(b)CF 2018 · para 4.62EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.1.1EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.1.3
  • Cost. Costs generally increase as the unit of account gets smaller, so rights or obligations from the same source are separated only if the information is more useful and the benefits outweigh the costs. CF 2018 · para 4.52EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.1.1
  • Same source — inseparable or separable. Interdependent rights and obligations that cannot be separated form a single asset or liability — for example, executory contracts; separable rights may be grouped separately from obligations, or grouped with them in one unit. A single unit of account differs from offsetting, which groups separately recognised assets and liabilities into one net amount. 4.7Executory contracts — one combined right and obligationtop

An executory contract (or portion) is equally unperformed and establishes a single combined right and obligation to exchange economic resources. Neither party has fulfilled any obligations, or both have partially fulfilled them to an equal extent. The right and obligation are interdependent and cannot be separated, so they are a single asset (terms currently favourable) or liability (terms currently unfavourable). Whether it is included in the statements depends on the recognition criteria and the measurement basis, including any onerous test. CF 2018 · para 4.56–4.57CF 2018 · para 4.53CF 2018 · para BC4.78PwC MOA 2020 · 1.70EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.1.1EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.1.2

An executory contract is neither a right plus a separate obligation nor a purchased option plus a written option. Leases and trade-date accounting apply the idea, recognition stays in Standards where often nothing is recognised, and a salary contract shows the link to past events.

  • Not a right plus a separate obligation. The right to receive one resource is conditional on transferring the other, and vice versa; even when transfers occur at different times, a simultaneous exchange occurs at the first transfer — when goods are delivered, the seller receives a right to payment and the customer an obligation to pay. CF 2018 · para BC4.79–BC4.81
  • Not a purchased option plus a written option. If an entity holds a purchased option and has written an identical option for the same exchange, each party’s right to withdraw is nullified — the exchange will occur unless both agree to terminate — so the contract is a single asset or liability. CF 2018 · para BC4.82–BC4.84
  • Leases and trade-date accounting. Once a lessor makes the underlying asset available, the lease is no longer executory: the lessee controls a right-of-use asset and has a liability for lease payments (IFRS 16 Basis). IFRS 9 trade-date accounting for regular-way purchases or sales is a simple and practical method justified by the cost constraint. CF 2018 · para BC4.85
  • Recognition stays in Standards — often nothing is recognised. The Framework sets no specific recognition requirements for executory contracts. In many cases no asset or liability is recognised and the Board expects that to continue; under historical cost an executory contract is typically measured at zero unless onerous — for example, a contract to buy inventories, assuming no transaction costs. CF 2018 · para BC4.86–BC4.87
  • Link to past events. A salary contract before the employee’s services are received is executory: a combined right and obligation to exchange future salary for future services, with no present obligation to pay salary yet. CF 2018 · para 4.47EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.3.3

Facts

  • Company C enters into a contract to buy equipment in six months and agrees to pay 100 at that time.
  • At inception, neither party has performed.

Assessment

  • Executory contract: seller must deliver equipment; buyer has the right to receive equipment and the obligation to pay 100; neither has performed.
  • Definition — a single asset or liability. The contract establishes a combined right and obligation to exchange economic resources; the right and obligation are interdependent and cannot be separated, so together they constitute a single asset or liability — an asset if the terms of the exchange are currently favourable, a liability if they are currently unfavourable.

  • Initially executory — not automatically a recognised asset/liability pair for the gross amounts under CF alone.

  • Executory status turns on performance symmetry — not a blank exclusion forever.

4.8Substance of contractual rights and obligationstop

Financial statements report the substance of the rights and obligations a contract creates. Sometimes the substance is clear from the legal form; in other cases the terms of a contract, or of a group or series of contracts, require analysis. These concepts draw on the Board’s standard-setting projects and are included so that they are applied more consistently in Standards. CF 2018 · para 4.59CF 2018 · para 2.12CF 2018 · para BC4.88EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 5.1.2EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.1.3 All terms with substance count, whether explicit or implicit, terms with no substance are disregarded, and a group or series of contracts can be one unit while one contract can be several.

  • All terms with substance count, explicit or implicit. Implicit terms could include obligations imposed by statute, such as statutory warranty obligations on entities selling goods to customers. CF 2018 · para 4.60EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.1.3
  • Terms with no substance are disregarded — terms with no discernible effect on the economics of the contract, such as (a) terms that bind neither party, or (b) rights, including options, that the holder will not have the practical ability to exercise in any circumstances. CF 2018 · para 4.61EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 7.1.3
  • A group or series of contracts can be one unit; one contract can be several. If rights or obligations in one contract merely nullify all those in another contract entered into at the same time with the same counterparty, the two contracts together create no rights or obligations. Conversely, a single contract creating sets of rights or obligations that could have been created through separate contracts may need to be accounted for as if they were separate contracts.
