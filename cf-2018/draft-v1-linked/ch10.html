Chapter 10 — Carve-out financial statements

Financial statements of a reporting entity that is a portion of an entity — a division or lesser business component carved out of a larger entity. The Framework allows a portion of an entity to be a reporting entity but does not use the term. CF 2018 · para 3.10PwC MOA 2020 · A2.5

Concept Map

10.0Scope of this chaptertop

Carve-out financial statements report on a portion of a larger entity — a division or lesser business component viewed as “carved out” of the larger consolidated financial statements. The Framework does not use the term; it allows a reporting entity to be a portion of an entity and sets the boundary tests that apply when the reporting entity is not a legal entity. CF 2018 · para 3.10CF 2018 · para 3.13–3.14PwC MOA 2020 · A2.5PwC MOA 2020 · A2.17KPMG Combined/Carve 2022 · 1.3 Official wording uses “portion of an entity” while “carve-out” is firm vocabulary, and a carve-out can also be combined.

10.1Reporting entity — a portion of an entitytop

A reporting entity can be a portion of an entity; when it is not a legal entity, primary users’ information needs set its boundary. CF 2018 · para 3.10CF 2018 · para 3.13–3.14EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2 A portion of an entity is allowed, but its boundary can be difficult and must pass faithful representation tests. Portions are why the Board tightened the boundary, and legal entities normally set it.

  • Portion allowed. A reporting entity can be a single entity, a portion of an entity, or more than one entity — not necessarily a legal entity. CF 2018 · para 3.10EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 1.2.90.10
  • Difficult boundary. If the reporting entity is not a legal entity and does not comprise only legal entities linked by a parent–subsidiary relationship, its boundary is driven by the information needs of its primary users. CF 2018 · para 3.13–3.14EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2
  • Faithful representation tests. The boundary must not contain an arbitrary or incomplete set of economic activities; including that set must give neutral information; and the statements must describe how the boundary was determined and what constitutes the reporting entity. See also 9.2 Difficult boundaries CF 2018 · para 3.14EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2KPMG Combined/Carve 2022 · 1.2
  • Portions are why the Board tightened the boundary. Respondents to the 2015 Exposure Draft worried that a portion of an entity could report an incomplete set of economic activities — for example, by excluding its share of overheads — and that identifying which claims to include could be difficult. The Board made completeness and neutrality particularly important in setting the boundary: an arbitrary or incomplete set gives information that is incomplete, may lack neutrality and does not meet users’ needs. CF 2018 · para BC3.17–BC3.18
  • Legal entities normally set the boundary. Determining the boundary is normally straightforward when the reporting entity is a legal entity, or comprises only legal entities all linked by parent–subsidiary relationships — the legal boundary then meets users’ needs. A portion of an entity falls outside that case. CF 2018 · para BC3.19

10.2Firm definitions — carve-outtop

Firms describe a carve-out as a division or lesser business component of a larger entity and as smaller components carved from a larger reporting entity, contrast it with combined financial statements, and note that it is rarely a neat sub-group.

  • A division or lesser business component of a larger entity. The term arises because the unit’s statements are viewed as “carved out” of the larger consolidated financial statements. There is no accounting definition of a carve-out transaction; the term is commonly used for equity carve-outs, spin-offs and split-offs. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.5PwC MOA 2020 · A2.17
  • Smaller components carved from a larger reporting entity. The term reflects that smaller components — e.g. unincorporated divisions — are carved out from a larger reporting entity. KPMG Combined/Carve 2022 · 1.3
  • Typical contrasts with combined financial statements. Generally not whole legal entities only; may lack full stand-alone historical ledgers; more pervasive allocations; may need parent support to operate. See also 9.5 KPMG Combined/Carve 2022 · 1.3
  • Rarely a neat sub-group. A new reporting entity is rarely a sub-group that has prepared consolidated statements presenting only and all of its activities — including debt, taxes, share-based payments, its share of corporate assets, and management services and charges. Most need adjustments, including allocating assets, liabilities and expenses that are relevant to the new reporting entity but not accounted for in the legal entities inside its boundary. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.65–A2.66

10.4Assets, debt and financingtop

The carve-out recognises all the assets its constituent entities control; attribution follows the legal substance of arrangements throughout the track-record period. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.79–A2.80 Corporate assets may or may not transfer, and assets can be attributed by ownership, usage or a sharing arrangement.

  • Corporate assets may or may not transfer. Challenges arise where the reporting entity used corporate assets such as brands or patents that might or might not transfer to it, or historically controlled assets that will not transfer as part of the transaction. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.79
  • Attribute by ownership, usage or a sharing arrangement. Assets might be attributed by legal ownership, by usage, or through an inter-company sharing arrangement such as an operating or lease agreement. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.80

Illustration: 10.4.1 — Brand owned by the parent: buyer acquires vs does not acquirePwC MOA 2020 · Exhibit A2.10 PwC MOA 2020 · A2.79–A2.80

Facts

  • A new reporting entity (business units planned for sale) and several other business units of the group used a brand name owned by the parent.
  • The brand name is patented and legally owned by the parent.
  • Should the brand be included in combined financial statements prepared in accordance with IFRS?

Assessment

  • Scenario 1 — the buyer does not acquire the brand. Including it as an asset is not appropriate, because the brand will not be sold to the buyer. A royalty the parent previously charged is included as an expense; if no royalty was charged, that fact is disclosed and no “as-if” adjustment is made. Consider whether any parent-level amortisation of the brand needs to be allocated.
  • Scenario 2 — the buyer acquires the brand. Including the parent’s historical cost of the brand is appropriate, with the related amortisation in the income statement.

Conclusion

  • Scenario 1: no brand asset; the historical royalty expense, or disclosure that none was charged.
  • Scenario 2: the brand at the parent’s historical cost — the carrying amount is frequently nil.

The choice between keeping and excluding a non-transferring asset may follow the regulator’s preference or the circumstances, and the same choice arises for disposal groups.

  • Assets that will not transfer — two approaches. Include the asset until the transaction date with clear disclosure that it will not transfer, or exclude it throughout the track-record period, again with clear disclosure. The regulator may prefer one, or one may suit the circumstances better; the same two approaches are seen for disposal groups. See also 9.7 disposal group PwC MOA 2020 · A2.81PwC MOA 2020 · A2.55

Debt is included where the reporting entity is legally obliged on it or where parent debt is assigned to or assumed by it, leases and interest follow the related items, and other debt is presented in the way most meaningful to users.

  • Debt the reporting entity is legally obliged on is included — including third-party debt issued directly by the legal entities that form the new reporting entity. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.82
  • Parent debt assigned to or assumed by the reporting entity is included for all periods in which it was outstanding. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.83
  • Leases and interest follow the related items. Lease liabilities follow the related right-of-use asset; interest income and costs follow the way the related debt has been apportioned. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.84
  • Other debt — present what is most meaningful to users. Debt may belong in the statements in other circumstances; interest-bearing obligations to the current parent are presented as debt financing, and the allocation basis should be consistent with the transaction, demerger or disposal agreement. 10.5Income and expenses — allocationstop

Include all economic activity of the business; income and expenses recorded elsewhere in the group are allocated when a reasonable basis can be established. A new reporting entity that has been part of a larger group will seldom have a free-standing track record. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.86–A2.87 Allocations are harder than for combined statements, corporate-level expenses need allocating by suitable methods, and allocating is not creating. Whether allocations count as historical or pro forma depends on the jurisdiction, and the factors usually considered include intra-group recharges.

  • Why harder than combined. Carve-outs often lack complete historical ledgers for the portion; shared assets, central services, and financing require allocation or attribution judgements. KPMG Combined/Carve 2022 · 1.3KPMG Combined/Carve 2022 · 4.2
  • Corporate-level expenses. Third-party expenses incurred centrally for lower-level entities include payroll, accounting, centralised purchasing, marketing, executive management, leases, advertising, legal, insurance, selling and administrative costs; pensions and other employee benefits may be recorded only at parent level. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.88
  • Allocation methods. Specific identification, legal obligation, or a basis that best reflects how the expense is incurred, such as headcount and time usage. Inter-company purchases and sales eliminated in the consolidated statements are reinstated. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.89–A2.90
  • Allocating is not creating. Estimating and allocating transactions not previously allocated differs from creating transactions that never happened, which is similar to pro forma information. Allocations are acceptable with an appropriate, clearly disclosed basis — how allocated items were identified, and the method and effect of allocation. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.91
  • Historical or pro forma depends on the jurisdiction. Some jurisdictions treat rational allocations of actual past group expenses as historical information; others treat any allocation as a pro forma adjustment — and pro forma information does not comply with IFRS. See also 9.7 PwC MOA 2020 · A2.67–A2.69
  • Factors usually considered. Whether the assets and liabilities will be part of the new reporting entity; whether there was any intra-group recharge (legal, accounting, finance or employee benefits); and whether recharges represent amounts actually incurred, and how their basis was determined. UK SIR 2000 and US SEC guidance may help. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.94

10.6Tax, interest, pensions and other specific areastop

IAS 12 applies to the carve-out even though its tax position rarely matches a stand-alone filer. The ideal — a legal sub-group filing group or individual returns that continues under the same tax regime — rarely occurs, so management analyses the tax circumstances, including legal status after the transaction. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.96–A2.97

Separate tax return method

Aggregates the tax positions of the individual entities. Entities that filed separate returns have a clear position: current tax follows the returns and deferred tax arises from individual book–tax differences. Where a group return allocates tax under a sharing arrangement, differences from tax paid or received are normally a capital contribution or distribution, included in the reconciliation of invested capital. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.98–A2.100

Tax incurred method

Might be appropriate if the parent recharged taxes to the entities that will form the new reporting entity. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.102

Deferred tax assets are recognised only for losses the new reporting entity can actually use, judged on its own results and the facts at each historical date.

  • Tax losses and deferred tax assets — no hindsight. Ensure tax losses carried forward will be available to the new reporting entity before recognising deferred tax assets. Assess recoverability using its results for all periods presented, with assumptions that reflect the facts at the historical dates. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.101PwC MOA 2020 · A2.103

Decision — is interest expense included?

Test

Has the carve-out historically been allocated interest, will it assume the debt, will offering proceeds repay the debt, or does parent debt relate specifically to its activity? PwC MOA 2020 · A2.104

Any

Outcome · Any applies

Include interest expense in the statements of the new reporting entity. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.104

None

Outcome · None applies

Not required, but usually allowed. Capital structures are usually complex; a regulator might not insist on interest, but usually allows a charge if a reasonable allocation can be made, whether or not interest was charged before. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.105

The nature of the plans after the transaction drives the pension treatment. The new reporting entity rarely has its own defined benefit plans, or the legal pension liability for its employees, at the time of the transaction. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.106–A2.107

Parent plan becomes a multi-employer plan

Contributions go to profit or loss of the new reporting entity, or employee benefit expense may be allocated to it. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.108

Portion of plan assets assigned

Pension assets and liabilities may be reflected, with disclosure, where the new reporting entity assumes the obligations for transferred employees — seldom effected by the transaction date, though likely announced or agreed; the calculation is complex and may need actuaries. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.109

The new reporting entity reaches its own impairment conclusions from its stand-alone assets and results rather than relying on the parent’s.

  • Impairment is tested on the carve-out’s own assets and results. The parent may have recorded impairments for assets in the new reporting entity, or avoided them because of group synergies that will no longer exist. The new reporting entity determines its own cash-generating units, tests goodwill and indefinite-life assets, and assesses triggers for its stand-alone operations in each period, without hindsight. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.110–A2.112
Pitfall: apportioning the parent’s impairment charges — impairments rest on the actual assets allocated to the carve-out entity and its stand-alone results. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.113

Further areas are share-based payments from the parent or a shareholder, which are IFRS 2 transactions, the reassessment of former group dealings, practical issues on separation, and shareholder-capacity transactions.

  • Share-based payments from the parent or a shareholder are IFRS 2 transactions. Awards to the new reporting entity’s employees by a related party or other holder of an economic interest, as compensation for services to the entity, are share-based payment transactions — unless clearly for another purpose, such as settling an obligation to the employee unrelated to employment. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.114
  • Former group dealings are reassessed. Interactions with other group entities are likely to be third-party transactions in the combined statements, while those entities are normally related parties during the track-record period, so IAS 24 applies. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.115–A2.116
  • Practical issues on separation. Trading balances may mix trading and financing elements that need separating; non-current classification may change; related-party receivables may not be recoverable once parental guarantees fall away; additional liabilities may crystallise. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.117
  • Shareholder-capacity transactions. Related-party and shareholder transactions in the carved entity may need special measurement and presentation; when the price is affected by the relationship, all aspects are identified and contributions from or distributions to holders of equity claims may be recognised. See also 6.7Illus 6.7.1 KPMG Combined/Carve 2022 · 4.2CF 2018 · para 6.80–6.82

10.7Equity, EPS, hindsight and cash flowstop

Equity is usually the parent’s net investment, legal share capital where it exists is shown separately, and balances with the parent that lack the characteristics of debt are part of invested capital.

  • Equity is usually the parent’s net investment. The new reporting entity is seldom a separate legal entity throughout the track record, so it has no share capital and reserves of its own; while separation is unfinished, equity is typically labelled “invested capital” or “parent company investment”. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.125
  • Legal share capital, where it exists, is shown separately — and EPS is then meaningful. Recyclable balances that other IFRSs require to be disclosed separately, such as hedging reserves, are disclosed whether or not legal accounts are shown. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.126–A2.127
  • Balances with the parent that lack the characteristics of debt are part of invested capital. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.128

EPS is required on a public filing but is often less meaningful without legal share capital. IAS 33 requires EPS for an entity that files, or is in the process of filing, with a securities regulator to issue ordinary shares in a public market. Some regulators accept omission; once shares are issued, EPS is adjusted retrospectively, and some regulators — notably the SEC — require the shares issued to existing investors on formation to be the historical denominator. Where invested capital fluctuates significantly, for example through share issuances for cash or a management buy-out, highlight the effect on the share count where practicable. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.129–A2.131 Preparing information on a combined basis can make EPS impossible to measure, as one prospectus extract explains.

  • Non-compliance with IAS 33 is disclosed. One prospectus extract states that, because the information is prepared on a combined basis, EPS cannot be measured and the IAS 33 requirement to disclose it has not been complied with. PwC MOA 2020 · Exhibit A2.15

A later authorisation date raises the hindsight question. The general principles are not to apply hindsight and to present the business as management ran it at the time. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.132

Three approaches (observed)

  • General (IAS 10): pack has its own authorisation date → 2020 and 2021 stay open through 25 Aug 2022 → recognise 10m expense + liability in 2020 comparative; 2021 carries liability with RE effect.

Conclusion

Inter-company cash flows appear at carve-out level, IFRS 1 applies to a new reporting entity, and some complex areas fall outside the guidance.

  • Inter-company cash flows appear at carve-out level. Inter-company transactions not reflected in the parent’s consolidated statement of cash flows should be reflected, and properly classified, in the carve-out entity’s statement of cash flows. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.133
  • IFRS 1 applies to a new reporting entity. See also 9.6 PwC MOA 2020 · A2.134
  • Areas outside the guidance. Complex areas that depend on individual facts or local regulation — for example, non-controlling interest and foreign currency translation differences — are not addressed. PwC MOA 2020 · A2.138

10.8Disclosure emphasistop

Describe how the boundary was determined and what constitutes the reporting entity — users need that description to understand what the statements include. CF 2018 · para 3.14CF 2018 · para BC3.18 Clear disclosure is fundamental for carve-outs and covers the allocation methodology, the effect of group tax arrangements on the tax charge, the effect of group financing on interest, and the carve-out basis narrative.

  • Clear disclosure is fundamental for carve-outs. Combined and carve-out information usually represents specific circumstances, often governed by local regulation; practice expands disclosure of the overall approach, accounting policies and estimates, and the basis of preparation — especially when allocations are pervasive. See also 9.7 minimum disclosure KPMG Combined/Carve 2022 · 5PwC MOA 2020 · A2.9PwC MOA 2020 · A2.93PwC MOA 2020 · A2.139
  • Allocation methodology. One annual report discloses its most significant allocations:
    • Group common services allocated mainly by percentage of revenue or employees
    • Capitalisation assumptions where liquid funds, loans and equity are not allocated by division — minor divisions assumed loan-financed, major divisions at the debt/equity ratio of their legal unit
    • Interest charged on average net assets
    PwC MOA 2020 · Exhibit A2.11
  • Group tax arrangements affect the tax charge. Tax in combined information may reflect group tax arrangements and is not necessarily representative of the charge as an independent group, or of future tax charges. PwC MOA 2020 · Exhibit A2.12
  • Group financing affects interest. Interest income and expense may reflect group financing arrangements, with rates set by the group — not necessarily representative of an independent group or of future interest. PwC MOA 2020 · Exhibit A2.13
  • Carve-out basis narrative. One demerger set describes its carve-out from the parent’s consolidated statements:
    • Management fees on the historical contractual cost-recovery basis
    • Group pension plans recognised at the contribution payable (IAS 19 group plans)
    • Interest on historical specific borrowings
    • Tax on a separate-return basis
    • Goodwill from earlier business combinations with parties outside the group
    • Group share schemes as equity-settled share-based payments — with the caveat that results are not necessarily representative of an autonomous entity
