Chapter 3 — Reporting entity

Concept Map

3.0Scope of this chaptertop

Chapter 3 moves from general purpose financial reports to general purpose financial statements — a particular form of those reports — and defines the reporting entity they are about. Chapters 1–2 discuss information in general purpose financial reports; Chapters 3–8 discuss information in general purpose financial statements, which provide information about the entity’s economic resources, claims and changes in them that meet the definitions of the elements (Table 4.1). Throughout the Framework, financial statements means general purpose financial statements. CF 2018 · para 3.1CF 2018 · para 3.1 fn7PwC MOA 2020 · 1.52EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6 The 2010 Framework never said what a reporting entity is or what it comprises, while the going concern wording changed only by replacing “curtail materially the scale of its operations” with “cease trading”.

  • 2018 Change — reporting entity concepts are new; going concern carried forward. The 2010 Framework did not discuss what a reporting entity is or how to determine what it comprises. The going concern description is brought forward from 2010 largely unchanged, except that cease trading replaces curtail materially the scale of its operations (see 3.4). CF 2018 · para BC3.11–BC3.12DTT A2 2022 · 6.1

Reporting entity

3.2Objective and scope of financial statements — reporting periodtop

Financial statements provide information about the elements that helps users assess cash-flow prospects and stewardship. The objective of financial statements is to provide financial information about the reporting entity’s assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses that is useful to users of financial statements in assessing the prospects for future net cash inflows to the reporting entity and in assessing management’s stewardship of the entity’s economic resources. CF 2018 · para 3.2CF 2018 · para 3.2 fn8EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.1.1 The information is provided in the statement of financial position, the statement(s) of financial performance and other statements and notes, the objective differs from IAS 1 by referring to elements rather than cash flows, and only the two statements that summarise recognised elements are named. Financial statements also give risk information on recognised and unrecognised items, cover a reporting period with comparatives, include forward-looking information only if it relates to the period’s elements and is useful, and include events after the reporting period when needed to meet the objective.

  • Where the information is provided.
    • (a) The statement of financial position — by recognising assets, liabilities and equity;
    • (b) The statement(s) of financial performance — by recognising income and expenses (the Framework does not specify one statement or two);
    • (c) Other statements and notes — by presenting and disclosing information about (i) recognised items, including their nature and risks; (ii) unrecognised assets and liabilities, including their nature and risks; (iii) cash flows; (iv) contributions from, and distributions to, holders of equity claims; and (v) methods, assumptions and judgements used in estimating amounts, and changes in them. CF 2018 · para 3.3CF 2018 · para 3.3 fn9PwC MOA 2020 · 1.53EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.1.1
  • Objective differs from IAS 1 — elements, not cash flows. To link to the elements, the Framework refers to assets, liabilities and equity (not “financial position”) and to income and expenses (not “financial performance”). It does not refer to cash-flow information: cash flows are important, but cash inflows and outflows are not elements. It also expands on usefulness — cash-flow prospects and stewardship. CF 2018 · para BC3.4
  • Why only two statements are named — they summarise recognised elements. Information about cash flows and about equity contributions and distributions is not less important; but only the statement of financial position and the statement(s) of financial performance summarise recognised elements and are where recognition occurs. CF 2018 · para BC3.6
  • Risk information — recognised and unrecognised items. Information about risks arising from recognised and unrecognised assets and liabilities is likely to be useful for assessing the entity’s ability to generate cash flows and management’s stewardship, so it contributes to the objective. CF 2018 · para 3.3(c)(i)–(ii)CF 2018 · para BC3.8EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.1.1
  • Reporting period and comparatives. Financial statements cover a specified period and give information about assets and liabilities — including unrecognised ones — and equity that existed at the end of, or during, the period, and income and expenses for the period. Comparative information for at least one preceding period helps users identify and assess changes and trends. CF 2018 · para 3.4–3.5PwC MOA 2020 · 1.54EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.1.2
  • Forward-looking information — only if it relates to the period’s elements and is useful. Included if it (a) relates to assets, liabilities (including unrecognised ones) or equity that existed at the end of or during the period, or to income or expenses for the period, and (b) is useful — for example, estimated future cash flows used to measure an asset or liability. Financial statements do not typically provide other forward-looking information, such as management’s expectations and strategies (see Ch2 · 2.0). CF 2018 · para 3.6PwC MOA 2020 · 1.55EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.1.2
  • Events after the reporting period — when needed to meet the objective. Information about transactions and other events after the period end is included if it is necessary to meet the objective of financial statements. CF 2018 · para 3.7PwC MOA 2020 · 1.56EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.1.2

3.3Perspective adopted — the reporting entity as a wholetop

Financial statements take the view of the reporting entity as a whole, which is separate from its capital providers. Financial statements provide information about transactions and other events viewed from the perspective of the reporting entity as a whole, not from the perspective of any particular group of the entity’s existing or potential investors, lenders or other creditors. CF 2018 · para 3.8EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.1.3 Under the entity perspective the reporting entity is separate from its investors, lenders and other creditors, and non-controlling interests are equity of the reporting entity.

  • Entity separate from investors, lenders and other creditors. The entity perspective reflects the Board’s view that the reporting entity is separate from its investors, lenders and other creditors. CF 2018 · para BC3.9
  • Non-controlling interests — equity of the reporting entity. As far as the reporting entity is concerned, non-controlling interests in a group have the characteristics of equity — a consequence of the entity perspective. DTT A2 2022 · 6.1CF 2018 · para 3.8EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.1.3

Entity perspective — adopted

Consistent with the objective in 1.2 — useful information for existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors as a group, rather than for a particular subset of capital providers. CF 2018 · para 3.8CF 2018 · para BC3.10EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.1.3

Perspective of a particular group — not adopted

Would need different statements for each subset. Directing information to one subset of primary users might require a different set of financial statements for each subset — which could cause confusion, undermine confidence in financial reporting and be expensive. CF 2018 · para BC3.10

3.4Going concern assumptiontop

Financial statements are normally prepared on the assumption that the reporting entity is a going concern and will continue in operation for the foreseeable future. Hence, it is assumed that the entity has neither the intention nor the need to enter liquidation or to cease trading. CF 2018 · para 3.9PwC MOA 2020 · 1.57DTT A2 2022 · 6.1EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.1.4 The going concern wording is aligned with IAS 1 and IAS 10, and its application sits in Standards.

  • Wording aligned with IAS 1 and IAS 10. Brought forward from the 2010 Framework largely unchanged; cease trading replaced curtail materially the scale of its operations to align the description more closely with IAS 1 and IAS 10. CF 2018 · para BC3.11DTT A2 2022 · 6.1
  • Application sits in Standards. The assumption is also addressed in IAS 1; Deloitte’s further going-concern guidance sits in its IAS 1 presentation chapter (A4, section 2.5), not in the Framework chapter. PwC MOA 2020 · 1.57DTT A2 2022 · 6.1

Facts

  • Company C is in liquidation — going-concern assumption does not apply for preparation of its financial statements. CF 2018 · para 3.9
  • C has a financial liability in the scope of IFRS 9.
  • Question: does leaving going concern allow immediate derecognition of that liability under CF alone?

Assessment

  • CF 3.9 addresses the preparation assumption / possible different basis — it does not rewrite IFRS 9 derecognition.
  • IFRS 9 still governs when a financial liability is derecognised (discharged, cancelled or expires).
  • Liquidation does not trigger derecognition — the IFRS 9 liability cannot be derecognised until IFRS 9 criteria are met, even though C is in liquidation.

Conclusion

  • Broken going concern ≠ automatic liability derecognition.
  • Apply the Standard for the item; CF Status — Standards prevail.
  • Related going-concern cases: post-reporting-date loss of going concern before authorisation → not going-concern basis; subsidiary non-GC vs parent GC → consolidate parent group on GC until disposal/liquidation (Insights 1.2.70.50–60).

3.5Reporting entitytop

A reporting entity is defined by preparing financial statements, not by legal form. A reporting entity is an entity that is required, or chooses, to prepare financial statements. A reporting entity can be a single entity or a portion of an entity or can comprise more than one entity. A reporting entity is not necessarily a legal entity. CF 2018 · para 3.10PwC MOA 2020 · 1.58EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6 Reporting entities are typically single legal structures or groups but can be portions of an entity, their financial statements fall into three kinds by composition, and the Framework describes a reporting entity rather than mandating who prepares statements.

  • Typical reporting entities — and portions of an entity. The most obvious examples are a single legal structure, such as an incorporated company, and a group comprising a parent and its subsidiaries; a reporting entity could also be a portion of a legal entity, such as a branch or the activities within a defined region. DTT A2 2022 · 6.2CF 2018 · para 3.10EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6
  • Three kinds of financial statements by composition. Parent and its subsidiaries → consolidated; the parent alone → unconsolidated; two or more entities not all linked by a parent–subsidiary relationship → combined (3.6, 3.7). CF 2018 · para 3.11–3.12PwC MOA 2020 · 1.59–1.61EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2
  • A description, not a mandate. The Framework describes a reporting entity rather than stating who must, should or could prepare general purpose financial statements — the Board has no authority to decide that. For the same reason it does not say when financial statements could be prepared on a carve-out basis (application in Ch10 Carve-out). CF 2018 · para BC3.13DTT A2 2022 · 6.2

3.6Reporting entity boundary — consolidated, unconsolidated and harder boundariestop

Legal entities usually set the boundary; where they do not, primary users’ information needs do. CF 2018 · para 3.11CF 2018 · para 3.13–3.14EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2

Decision — consolidated or unconsolidated financial statements

Test

Where a parent controls subsidiaries — does the reporting entity comprise both the parent and its subsidiaries, or the parent alone? CF 2018 · para 3.11

Both

Outcome · Parent and subsidiaries

Consolidated financial statements — parent and subsidiaries as a single reporting entity. CF 2018 · para 3.11CF 2018 · para 3.15

Parent

Outcome · Parent alone

Unconsolidated financial statements — the parent’s own assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses. CF 2018 · para 3.11CF 2018 · para 3.17

Consolidated statements help assess the parent’s cash-flow prospects, unconsolidated statements are useful but not a substitute, and the Framework’s “unconsolidated” is IAS 27’s “separate”.

  • Consolidated — useful for cash-flow prospects of the parent. Information about parent and subsidiaries as a single reporting entity helps the parent’s investors, lenders and other creditors assess prospects for future net cash inflows to the parent, because those inflows include distributions from subsidiaries, which depend on the subsidiaries’ own net cash inflows. Consolidated statements are not designed to give separate information about any particular subsidiary — the subsidiary’s own statements do that; an item may be material to a subsidiary’s statements but not to the parent’s consolidated statements. CF 2018 · para 3.15–3.16CF 2018 · para BC3.25PwC MOA 2020 · 1.59EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2.1
  • Unconsolidated — useful, but not a substitute. Parent-alone information can be useful because (a) a claim against the parent typically gives no claim against the subsidiaries, and (b) in some jurisdictions, amounts legally distributable to the parent’s equity holders depend on the parent’s distributable reserves. Parent-alone information can also be given in the notes to the consolidated statements. Unconsolidated statements are typically not sufficient for the parent’s investors, lenders and other creditors, so they cannot substitute for required consolidated statements; a parent may still prepare them in addition. CF 2018 · para 3.17–3.18PwC MOA 2020 · 1.60EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2.1
  • Terminology — the Framework’s “unconsolidated” is IAS 27’s “separate”. The Framework calls the parent’s own statements unconsolidated financial statements; IAS 27 and other Standards use separate financial statements. CF 2018 · para 3.11DTT A2 2022 · 6.2

Boundary driven by primary users’ needs — relevant information that faithfully represents: (a) not an arbitrary or incomplete set of economic activities; (b) including that set results in neutral information; (c) a description of how the boundary was determined and what constitutes the reporting entity. CF 2018 · para 3.13–3.14DTT A2 2022 · 6.2EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 1.2.90.30–40

An arbitrary collection of assets and liabilities, such as a portion of an entity excluding its share of overheads, could give incomplete and misleading information, so the Board required a description of how the boundary was set and what the reporting entity comprises.

  • Why completeness and neutrality matter most for the boundary. Respondents to the 2015 Exposure Draft were concerned that statements could be prepared for any arbitrary collection of assets and liabilities — for example, a portion of an entity excluding its share of overheads — giving incomplete, misleading information, and that it may be hard to identify which claims belong to a portion. The Board therefore stressed completeness and neutrality, and required a description of how the boundary was set and what the reporting entity comprises. CF 2018 · para BC3.17–BC3.18DTT A2 2022 · 6.2

3.7Combined financial statementstop

Combined financial statements are acknowledged, but the Framework does not say when or how to prepare them. The points are the definition and status, that they are not limited to common control, and the sibling-subsidiaries example. CF 2018 · para 3.12CF 2018 · para BC3.21EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2

  • Definition and status. Two or more entities not all linked by a parent–subsidiary relationship → combined financial statements. The Framework acknowledges but does not develop when or how (see Sources differ — combined financial statements and carve-out) — application densified in Chapter 9 Combined FS and Chapter 10 Carve-out. CF 2018 · para 3.12DTT A2 2022 · 6.2CF 2018 · para BC3.20–BC3.21EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2KPMG Insights 2019/20 · 1.2.90.20KPMG Combined/Carve 2022 · 1.3
  • Not limited to common control. The 2010 Exposure Draft said combined statements might be useful for entities under common control; many commenters welcomed the discussion but disagreed with restricting combined statements to entities under common control. The Board concluded that combined statements can be useful in some circumstances and left when and how to a possible future Standard. CF 2018 · para BC3.20–BC3.21EY iGAAP 2026 Ch2 · section 6.2
  • Example — sibling subsidiaries. Combined financial statements of sibling subsidiaries within a group are an example; the Board concluded the concept is best developed as a separate standard-setting project rather than in the Framework. DTT A2 2022 · 6.2
