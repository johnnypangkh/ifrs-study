3.0 Scope — five P&L categoriestop

Classify every P&L amount into one of five categories. IFRS 18 requires an entity to classify income and expenses included in the statement of profit or loss in the operating, investing, financing, income taxes or discontinued operations category. Operating is the residual/default. Investing and financing follow specified asset and liability logic, with overrides when investing in assets or providing financing to customers is a specified main business activity. Income taxes and discontinued operations close the set. Foreign exchange differences, the net monetary position, derivatives and derecognition follow the cross-category rules in 3.7. , (selective) IFRS 18 · para 47–68 IFRS 18 · para B29–B72 Classifying an amount across the five P&L categories takes three decisions (which band, whether there is a specified MBA, and whether the amount is residual operating), and general and specific requirements apply together.

  • Second decision — specified MBA? Assess whether investing in assets or providing financing to customers is a main business activity before applying the investing and financing tests. If yes, some amounts that would otherwise sit in investing or financing are classified in operating instead. IFRS 18 · para 49–51IFRS 18 · para 55–58IFRS 18 · para 65–66

An entity with a specified main business activity also applies the specific requirements, but only to the income and expenses they cover; the rest of its income and expenses stay under the general requirements. Specific requirements also exist for issuers of insurance contracts and of investment contracts with participation features. 3.2 Operating — the default categorytop

Operating is residual by design. An entity shall classify in the operating category all income and expenses included in the statement of profit or loss that are not classified in investing, financing, income taxes or discontinued operations. IFRS 18 · para 52 Operating is the default category and gives a complete operations picture, as App B confirms, with operations including supporting activities. The category is residual rather than directly defined because a direct definition would be hard to apply consistently, the Big4 align on it, and it links to operating profit.

  • Complete operations picture. The Introduction states that the operating category is the default and provides a complete picture of an entity’s operations for the reporting period; it includes, but is not limited to, income and expenses from main business activities regardless of whether they are volatile or non-recurring. IFRS 18 · para IN9
  • App B confirmation. B42 repeats that operating includes all income and expenses not classified in the other categories applying paragraphs 53–68, including such income or expenses that are volatile or non-recurring, and that main-business results land in operating except equity-accounted investments. IFRS 18 · para B42
  • Operations include supporting activities. In the Board’s view all income and expenses not relating to another category arise from operations, and operations are not limited to main business activities: supporting activities that do not generate revenue directly also give rise to operating income and expenses. Volatile, unusual or non-recurring items are included because faithful representation of the period’s operations needs them — omitting either the depreciation or the impairment of an item of equipment would not depict the decision to buy and deploy it. IFRS 18 · para BC89 EY Closer look 2026 · section 3.2.3 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 3.2.3
  • Why residual, not a direct definition. BC91 explains that a direct definition of operating profit would be difficult to apply consistently for all entities; classifying investing and financing first, then treating operating as default, is expected to be simpler and more consistent. IFRS 18 · para BC91
  • Big4 alignment. KPMG describes operating as a ‘default’ or ‘residual’ category and stresses that volatile, unusual or non-recurring items are not excluded; EY likewise positions operating as residual so that main-business items (and supporting activities) stay inside operating, including volatile items. Equity-accounted results remain in investing even if management views them as main-business. KPMG FI 2024 · section 2.1.2.1 EY Closer look 2026 · section 3.2.3 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 3.2.3

Facts

  • Entity A receives a government grant to purchase a new rolling mill, which it uses in combination with other assets to produce goods. It recognises deferred income and releases it over the mill’s useful life; its policy presents grant income gross.
  • Entity B receives a government grant to reimburse the interest expense on its loan with Bank X. The grant is received in full when the loan is taken out, recognised as deferred income and released as the loan interest is paid; its policy also presents grant income gross.

Assessment

  • Entity A. The income is the release of deferred income, not a return from the mill (an asset used with others). It does not arise from a Type 1 liability and is not interest identified on a Type 2 liability.
  • Entity B. The income is not from a stand-alone-return asset; it is not part of the initial or subsequent amortised-cost accounting for the Bank X loan under IFRS 9, so not Type 1 income; and it is not identified interest on a Type 2 liability.

Decision

  • Gross policy — both entities: classify the grant income in operating.
  • Net policy — Entity A: the grant reduces depreciation on the mill, which stays in operating.
  • Net policy — Entity B: the grant reduces interest expense on the Type 1 Bank X loan, which is classified in financing.
  • Gross policy in practice: EY sees gross grant income as generally operating, but practice may develop and should be monitored.

3.3 Investing categorytop

Investing separates stand-alone returns from operations. Except as required by paragraphs 55–58 for an entity that has a specified main business activity, classify in investing the paragraph 54 income and expenses from the assets listed in paragraph 53. IFRS 18 · para 53 The investing category captures three asset sets (paragraph 53) and moves specified amounts (paragraph 54), distinguishing stand-alone from combination assets. The band exists so investors can analyse stand-alone investment returns separately from operations, some assets keep their own rules, and the Big4 align on the approach.

  • Three asset sets (paragraph 53).
    1. Associates, joint ventures and unconsolidated subsidiaries (B43–B44).
    2. Cash and cash equivalents.
    3. Other assets that generate a return individually and largely independently of the entity’s other resources (B45–B49).
    IFRS 18 · para 53
  • What amounts move (paragraph 54). For those assets, classify in investing:
    • (a) The income generated by the assets;
    • (b) Income and expenses from initial and subsequent measurement, including on derecognition; and
    • (c) Incremental expenses directly attributable to acquiring and disposing of the assets (e.g. transaction costs, costs to sell)

3.3.3 — Cash and cash equivalents

Cash and cash equivalents are investing by default, and purpose does not matter without a specified MBA. Money market funds, interest on customer advances and the exceptions need specific treatment.

  • Investing by default. Income and expenses from cash and cash equivalents are investing, to keep the statement easier to understand and because cash generates returns individually and largely independently.   • This entails assessing whether these items have shared characteristics, and whether presenting them together provides a useful structured summary, as discussed below.
  • Shared characteristics In order to present these items in the same line item, they must have at least one shared characteristic other than meeting the definition of assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses or cash flows.
  • Paragraph B78 of IFRS 18 provides examples of such characteristics.
  • It is generally more straightforward to demonstrate shared characteristics between gains and losses on designated hedging instruments and the income and expenses of the hedged item, compared to derivatives used for economic hedging purposes.
  • This is because hedge accounting under IFRS 9 requires: An economic relationship between the hedging instrument and the hedged item (that is, their values move in opposite directions because of the same risk), supporting the existence of shared characteristics.
  • Timing alignment through hedge accounting mechanics, ensuring that gains and losses on the hedging instrument are recognised in profit or loss when the hedged item affects profit or loss.
  • No such requirements exist for economic hedging, and so it might be more difficult to demonstrate shared characteristics – particularly where there is a substantive difference in timing between when the derivative gains and losses are recognised and when the related income and expenses are recognised in profit or loss.
  • Useful structured summary In order to present these items in the same line item, such presentation must provide a useful structured summary in accordance with paragraph 16 of IFRS 18.
  • If income and expenses from the item being managed are required to be presented in a line item in accordance with paragraph 75 of IFRS 18 (such as revenue), this requires an additional assessment of whether separate presentation of the required line item is necessary for the statement of profit or loss to provide a useful structured summary (see FAQ 18.33.1).
  • Other considerations Where an entity disaggregates a portion of the total gain or loss on such instruments (for example, interest income/expenses on interest-related derivatives or realised gains or losses on non-interest related derivatives), the relevant guidance in FAQ 18.38.2, FAQ 18.36.1, and FAQ 18.36.2 should be applied.
  • If the requirements for aggregation in IFRS 18 are met, the aggregated line item should be labelled and described in a way that faithfully represents the characteristics of the aggregated items.

3.10.42 — In which line item should an entity present the gains or losses recognised in a subsequent period…

In which line item should an entity present the gains or losses recognised in a subsequent period… PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.93.1 IFRS 18 · para 42

  • An entity might recognise gains and losses related to the disposal of a business in financial reporting periods after the initial gain or loss on disposal was recognised.
  • Question In which line item should an entity present the gains or losses recognised in a subsequent period to the initial gain or loss on disposal of a business?
  • Answer Typically these would be presented in the same line in which the initial gain or loss was presented in an earlier year, whether as part of continuing or discontinued operations.
  • The gain or loss recognised subsequent to the disposal is typically an intrinsic part of the disposal transaction (impacting the overall profitability of the transaction) and will share the same characteristics as the gain or loss initially recognised on disposal.
  • It would be misleading to not present these amounts in the same line (even though they are recognised in different periods).
  • Illustrative example A manufacturing entity sold a business on 31 December 20X1, the last day of the accounting period.
  • The business that was sold leased certain properties.
  • The lessor has recourse to the entity under the terms of the lease for the unpaid rentals for a specified period of the lease.
  • The entity recognised the recourse arrangement as a financial guarantee and recorded the liability at its fair value of C100,000 at the date of disposal, reducing the gain on disposal shown in the statement of profit or loss from C900,000 to C800,000.
  • The financial liability was remeasured at 31 December 20X2, and was stated at C80,000 in the statement of financial position at that date.
  • The purchaser of the business defaulted on the last lease payment due in the specified period, which has now expired; so, in 20X3, the entity was called on to pay C85,000.
  • The overall gain/loss on disposal of the business over the three years is C815,000, being the C900,000 ‘pre-guarantee gain’ less C85,000 paid out under the guarantee.

Illustration: 3.10.49 — Classification of gains and losses on share purchase warrants held by an entity PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · EX 18.86.7 IFRS 18 · para 48 IFRS 18 · para B72

Facts

  • Background Company A holds share purchase warrants.
  • These give company A the option to purchase 1,000 shares in company B for CU5 per share at any time prior to 15 January 20X9.
  • The exercise of the warrants would give Company A significant influence over company B and company A would thereafter apply equity accounting to the associate.
  • Company A purchased the share purchase warrants as part of a planned strategic investment in company B in order to mitigate the risk of an increase in company B’s share price before company A acquired its shareholding.
  • Company A accounts for the share purchase warrants as financial assets at fair value through profit and loss, because they meet the definition of a derivative under IFRS 9.
  • The share purchase warrants are assets that can generate a return individually and largely independently of other resources of company A, since they can be sold to third parties or exercised for cash for a value that is more (or less) than the purchase price paid for the warrants.
  • Question Should company A classify fair value gains and losses on the share purchase warrants in the investing category?
  • Answer Yes, because in this example the warrants are used to manage an identified risk relating to the purchase of an associate.

Assessment

  • The share purchase warrants are derivative instruments, and so the guidance in IFRS 18 on the classification of gains and losses on derivative instruments applies.
  • This is the case even where the derivatives also meet the definition of an asset that generates a return individually and largely independently of other resources of the entity.
  • This view is confirmed by paragraph BC233 of the Basis of Conclusions on IFRS 18, which states: “A derivative not used to manage exposures to identified risks and not related to transactions that involve only the raising of finance will generally generate returns individually and largely independently of an entity’s other resources.
  • The IASB considered whether to require an entity to classify fair value gains and losses on such derivatives in the investing category, but decided not to do so”.
  • In determining the appropriate presentation of gains and losses on share purchase warrants and other similar derivatives the entity would consider: a. whether the warrants are used to manage identified risks (refer to FAQ 18.86.4); and b. the category within the statement of profit and loss in which the related income and expenses affected by those identified risks are recognised.
  • In this example, the share purchase warrants were purchased to manage an identified risk – that is, the risk that the cash flows required to purchase an interest in an associate might increase in the future.
  • The cost of the investment in the associate at initial recognition affects the income and expenses that ultimately arise from the subsequent measurement of the associate; and the income and expenses are presented within the investing category when they occur.
  • Therefore, the gains and losses on the share purchase warrants will also be recognised in the investing category.
