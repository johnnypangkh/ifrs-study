7.1 Statement of cash flows — operating profit start and interest and dividend classificationtop

Amended IAS 7 requires all entities using the indirect method to start operating cash flows from the operating profit or loss subtotal (IAS 7 paragraph 18(b)). The Board wanted to reduce diversity in practice: a common starting point makes statements of cash flows more consistent, helps investors compare operating cash flows, and removes some reconciling items used today. IAS 7 as amended IFRS 18 · para 70 EY Closer look 2026 · section 5.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 5.1 KPMG FI 2024 · section 5.1 IAS 7 now starts from operating profit through a Standard bridge that affects most entities, and it changes how interest and dividends are classified. P&L categories are not IAS 7 activities, and the IE cover the change.

  • Standard bridge. Operating profit (paragraphs 69–70) is the defined operations subtotal, so opening the statement of cash flows from it matches the P&L operating band. IFRS 18 · para 69–70
  • Impact on most entities. Pre-amendment IAS 7 required “profit or loss” as the starting point without defining it, so entities start from profit before tax or profit after tax. EY’s view: the change will affect most, if not all, entities. EY Closer look 2026 · section 5.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 5.1
  • Interest and dividends. The amendments remove the classification options for interest and dividend cash flows, because today’s mixed classifications do not necessarily show the role of interest and dividends in an entity’s business. Dividends paid are financing for all entities, as a cost of obtaining financing (IAS 7 paragraph 33A); see 7.1.1 and 7.1.2. KPMG FI 2024 · section 5.1 EY Closer look 2026 · section 5.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 5.1 KPMG FI 2024 · section 5.1.1
  • P&L categories are not IAS 7 activities. The IFRS 18 categories and the IAS 7 activities have different definitions: the Board put each statement’s objective ahead of aligning them. For example, cash from selling property, plant and equipment is an investing cash flow, but the gain or loss is in the operating category because the asset is used with the entity’s other resources and does not generate a return individually. IFRS 18 · para BC86 KPMG FI 2024 · section 2.1.2.2 EY Closer look 2026 · section 5.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 5.1
  • IE coverage. IE Part I does not illustrate a statement of cash flows; the worked statement in Illustration 7.1.3 is EY’s, based on Illustrative Example A accompanying amended IAS 7. IFRS 18 · para IE6(a) EY Closer look 2026 · section 5.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 5.1

7.1.2 — Specified main business activities — one activity per cash flow type

Cash flows follow the P&L, each is assessed separately, an income or expense split across P&L categories needs care, and dividends paid have their own classification.

  • Follow the P&L. An entity that invests in assets or provides financing to customers as a main business activity classifies the total of interest paid, the total of interest received and the total of dividends received each in a single activity, matching how the related expense or income is classified in the P&L (IAS 7 paragraphs 34B–34C). EY Closer look 2026 · section 5.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 5.1 KPMG FI 2024 · section 5.1.1
  • Assessed separately. The three flows do not have to land in the same activity: an entity investing in financial assets can classify all dividends received in operating (matching dividend income) and all interest paid in financing (matching interest expense). EY Closer look 2026 · section 5.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 5.1
  • Income or expense split across P&L categories. If, say, interest expense sits in both the operating and the financing category, the entity makes an accounting policy choice to classify all interest paid as either operating or financing (IAS 7 paragraph 34D, quoted by EY). EY Closer look 2026 · section 5.1 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 5.1 KPMG FI 2024 · section 5.1.1
  • Dividends paid. Financing when they relate to equity instruments, whatever the P&L shows; when they relate to instruments classified as liabilities, the interest-paid guidance applies. KPMG FI 2024 · section 5.1.1

7.2 Statement of financial position — limited changes and the structured-summary lenstop

Current and non-current classification and the minimum line items are largely carried forward from IAS 1; IFRS 18 adds a goodwill line item and requires nature and function to drive SFP aggregation. Paragraph 96 requires separate current and non-current classifications unless a liquidity-based presentation gives a more useful structured summary, in which case all assets and liabilities are presented in order of liquidity. IFRS 18 · para 96 IFRS 18 · para 104 IFRS 18 · para 15–24 IFRS 18 · para 41–45 SFP changes are limited, as the XYZ conclusion (IE7) shows. Goodwill is separate from intangible assets, nature and function apply, the line-item list was not revisited, and aggregation applies on the SFP.

  • XYZ conclusion (IE7). A statement of financial position distinguishing current from non-current items provides the most useful structured summary of its assets and liabilities. IFRS 18 · para IE7(c) IFRS 18 · para 96

  • Nature and function. Assets and liabilities are aggregated and disaggregated using nature and function; other characteristics (duration, liquidity, measurement basis, type, tax effects) help identify them. 7.3 EPS — permitted numerators for additional amounts per sharetop

Amended IAS 33 still permits additional amounts per share, but only when the numerator is a total or subtotal identified in IFRS 18 (paragraphs 69, 86 and 118) or an MPM, attributable to ordinary equity holders of the parent (IAS 33 paragraph 73B). The IASB specified the numerators so that users get the same information about additional per-share amounts as they get about MPMs. IAS 33 as amended EY Closer look 2026 · section 5.3 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 5.3 KPMG FI 2024 · section 5.3 Additional amounts per share use the same denominator with equal prominence and appear in the notes only. An MPM numerator brings MPM disclosures, a non-compliant numerator is not allowed, amounts required by local law or regulation can qualify, and the rules differ from before.

  • Same denominator, equal prominence. Additional basic and diluted amounts use the weighted-average number of ordinary shares determined under IAS 33 and are disclosed with equal prominence (IAS 33 paragraph 73C(a)–(b)). EY Closer look 2026 · section 5.3 EY iGAAP 2026 Ch04 · section 5.3 KPMG FI 2024 · section 5.3
  • Notes only. 7.5 Capital disclosures — paragraphs 126–129 and IE Part IIItop

An entity discloses information that enables users to evaluate its objectives, policies and processes for managing capital, based on information provided internally to key management personnel. These requirements are carried forward from IAS 1. The IASB put them in IAS 1 rather than IFRS 7 because capital does not relate solely to financial instruments; they are illustrated by EE Group and FF Group in IE Part III. IFRS 18 · para IE14–IE17 IFRS 18 · para 126–128 IFRS 18 · para BCZ394 Capital disclosures apply to all entities, and paragraph 127 sets out what is required. Capital need not equal equity, separate requirements may need disclosing one by one, and disclosure starts with a discussion.

  • All entities. Capital information is useful for all entities, not only those with external requirements: the level of capital and how it is managed affect the risk profile, resilience to unexpected adverse events and the ability to pay dividends. 7.6.1 — How should the most relevant presentation (current/non-current or liquidity presentation) of the…

How should the most relevant presentation (current/non-current or liquidity presentation) of the… PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.125.1 IFRS 18 · paras 96, paras B90-B92

  • Entities need to determine whether a current/non-current classified or a liquidity presentation of the statement of financial position would provide the more useful structured summary.
  • Most industrial and retail entities would present a current/non-current classified statement of financial position.
  • This is because significant amounts of their assets and liabilities would be realised or settled within a relatively short period.
  • Property developers might be less likely to use a current/non-current classified statement of financial position (as their assets and liabilities might be realised or settled over a long period, sometimes over several years), unless a clearly identifiable operating cycle exists.
  • It is common practice to consider a normal operating cycle to be a year but the period of one year after the end of the reporting period is not an inflexible cut-off point for determining the current/non-current status of assets and liabilities.
  • An asset recoverable within the normal operating cycle of more than one year is still a current asset; a liability to be settled within the normal operating cycle of more than one year is still a current liability.
  • A liquidity presentation might be more appropriate where the operating assets and liabilities are recovered or settled over very long periods.
  • For example, while a property development entity might have a clearly identifiable operating cycle, in some cases it might not be sensible to present development properties (held as inventory) and debtors as current assets if the development properties are only likely to be realised several years after the end of the reporting period.
  • A current/non-current classified statement of financial position might not be the most useful form of presentation even where assets and liabilities are readily realisable or capable of immediate settlement.
  • For example, investment companies typically have such assets and liabilities, but the timing of their realisation or settlement is dependent not just on their liquidity, but also on investment managers’ judgements on expected movements in market prices.
  • The distinction between current and non-current items is not particularly meaningful in such cases.

7.6.2 — How should the ordinary operating cycle be determined?

How should the ordinary operating cycle be determined? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.128.1 IFRS 18 · para B95

  • 1 The time between the first purchase of goods and the realisation of those goods in cash is 16 months (10 months + 6 months).
  • The age of inventory held by the entity at the year end will range between zero months to 10 months.
  • Once the goods are delivered, it will take a further six months to receive payment.
  • All of the inventory should be classified as a current asset, even though some of the inventory will not be realised in cash within 12 months of the reporting period.
  • This is because the inventory is realised in the entity’s normal operating cycle.
  • However, the entity should disclose the expected date of recovery of the inventory.
  • The trade receivables will be realised in cash within 12 months of the reporting period and are included as a current asset.
  • Question 2 Would the answer to Question 1 be different if the production time was 14 months, and the time between delivery and payment was a further 15 months?
  • The inventory and trade receivables would still be classified as current assets.
  • In this case, the inventory is, on average, older but it is still realised in cash within the entity’s normal operating cycle.
  • Similarly, the trade receivables are realised in cash as part of the operating cycle and are classified as a current asset, even though they will not be realised in cash within 12 months of the year end.
  • The entity should disclose the expected date of recovery of the inventory and the maturity date of the trade receivables.

7.6.3 — How should an inter-company receivable due on demand be classified?

How should an inter-company receivable due on demand be classified? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.128.2 IFRS 18 · paras 99, 101

  • A parent provides a loan to a subsidiary.
  • Interest of 8% is paid annually.
  • The loan is repayable on demand.
  • How should the loan receivable be classified in the parent’s statement of financial position?
  • Answer The demand feature might be primarily a form of protection or a tax-driven feature of the loan.
  • Both parties might expect and intend that the loan will remain outstanding for the foreseeable future.
  • If so, the instrument is, in substance, long-term in nature, and the parent classifies it as a non-current asset.
  • If, however, the parent expects that it will be repaid within 12 months of the reporting period, the parent classifies it as a current asset.
  • The subsidiary classifies the liability as current, because it does not have the right to defer repayment for more than 12 months, regardless of the parties’ expectations.

7.6.4 — Should derivative assets be classified as current or non-current?

Should derivative assets be classified as current or non-current? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.130.1 IFRS 18 · paras 99, para B95, BCZ287

  • Paragraph B95 of IFRS 18 clarifies that some, rather than all, financial assets that meet the definition of ‘held for trading’ in accordance with Appendix A to IFRS 9 are current assets.
  • Derivatives that are not held primarily for the purpose of trading are not required to be classified as current simply because they fall within the ‘held for trading’ definition in IFRS 9.
  • Rather, the requirements of paragraph 99 of IFRS 18 should be applied in determining classification.
  • Derivatives expected to be realised or intended to be sold or consumed in the entity’s normal operating cycle Such a derivative is classified as current in its entirety.
  • See paragraph 18.125 for guidance related to classification of items as working capital of the entity.
  • Derivatives held primarily for the purpose of trading in accordance with paragraph 99(b) of IFRS 18 Such a derivative is classified as current in its entirety.
  • Derivatives neither expected to be realised or intended to be sold or consumed in the entity’s normal operating cycle nor held primarily for the purpose of trading in accordance with paragraph 99(b) of IFRS 18 A derivative, whose final maturity is within 12 months after the end of the reporting period, is classified as current in its entirety.
  • A derivative, whose final maturity is later than 12 months after the end of the reporting period and for which no settlement will occur within 12 months after the end of the reporting period, is generally classified as non-current in its entirety.
  • However, it is classified as current if the entity expects to realise the derivative asset within 12 months after the end of the reporting period.
  • It is less clear how an entity classifies a derivative whose final maturity is later than 12 months after the end of the reporting period where that derivative contract has periodic cash settlements, including some of which occur within 12 months after the end of the reporting period – for example, an interest rate swap with a final maturity later than 12 months after the end of the reporting period and quarterly cash settlements.
  • We believe that there are two acceptable approaches to classifying such derivatives: I.
  • Classify the portion of the derivative expected to be settled within 12 months after the end of the reporting period as current, and the portion expected to be settled later than 12 months after the end of the reporting period as non-current.

7.6.5 — Can an entity present deferred tax as neither current nor non-current?

Can an entity present deferred tax as neither current nor non-current? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.132.1 IFRS 18 · paras 98, para B92

  • Deferred tax assets and liabilities cannot be classified as current.
  • We consider that entities presenting a current/non-current classified statement of financial position should not show deferred tax assets and deferred tax liabilities as separate categories; that is, they should not show deferred tax assets as neither current nor non-current, with the same approach adopted for deferred tax liabilities.
  • IFRS 18 states that a mixed presentation might be appropriate in certain circumstances but deferred tax assets and liabilities arise in the financial statements of most entities.
  • In our view, it is not the standard’s intention that the use of mixed presentation would be available or appropriate to all entities.

7.6.6 — Should derivative liabilities be classified as current or non-current?

Should derivative liabilities be classified as current or non-current? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.134.1 IFRS 18 · paras 101, para B97, BCZ287

  • Paragraph B97 of IFRS 18 clarifies that some, rather than all, financial liabilities that meet the definition of ‘held for trading’ in accordance with Appendix A to IFRS 9 are current liabilities.
  • Derivatives that are not held primarily for the purpose of trading are not required to be classified as current simply because they fall within the ‘held for trading’ definition in IFRS 9.
  • Rather, the requirements of paragraph 101 of IFRS 18 should be applied in determining classification.
  • Derivatives expected to be settled within the entity’s normal operating cycle Such a derivative is classified as current in its entirety.
  • See paragraph 18.125 for guidance related to classification of items as working capital of the entity.
  • Derivatives held primarily for the purpose of trading in accordance with paragraph 101(b) of IFRS 18 Such a derivative is classified as current in its entirety.
  • Derivatives neither expected to be settled within the entity’s normal operating cycle nor held primarily for the purpose of trading in accordance with paragraph 101(b) of IFRS 18 A derivative, whose final maturity is within 12 months after the end of the reporting period, is generally classified as current in its entirety.
  • However, it is classified as non-current if the entity has a right at the end of the reporting period to defer its settlement for more than 12 months after the end of the reporting period.
  • A derivative, whose final maturity is later than 12 months after the end of the reporting period and for which no settlement will occur within 12 months after the end of the reporting period, is classified as non-current in its entirety.
  • It is less clear how an entity classifies a derivative whose final maturity is later than 12 months after the end of the reporting period where that derivative contract has periodic cash settlements, including some of which occur within 12 months after the end of the reporting period – for example, an interest rate swap with a final maturity later than 12 months after the end of the reporting period and quarterly cash settlements.
  • We believe that there are two acceptable approaches to classifying such derivatives: I.
  • Classify the portion of the derivative due to be settled within 12 months after the end of the reporting period as current, and the portion due to be settled later than 12 months after the end of the reporting period as non-current.

7.6.7 — Should puttable debt that is puttable by the holder within the next 12 months be classified as…

Should puttable debt that is puttable by the holder within the next 12 months be classified as… PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.134.2 IFRS 18 · para 101

  • An entity has puttable debt that is puttable by the holder within the next 12 months, but, if not put, is repayable only beyond 12 months.
  • Should it be classified as current or non-current?
  • Answer The puttable debt should be classified in its entirety as current.
  • This is irrespective of whether IFRS 9 requires the put option to be accounted for as a separate embedded derivative and, if it does, irrespective of whether the host debt contract is reported in a separate statement of financial position line item from the embedded derivative.
  • This reflects that the put option could cause the entire instrument to be settled in a manner that is regarded as a liability under IAS 32.

7.6.8 — How should a liability with a contingent settlement condition be classified under the current or…

How should a liability with a contingent settlement condition be classified under the current or… PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.134.4 IFRS 18 · para 101(d)

  • The guidance to be applied to determine the classification of such a liability depends on whether the arrangement is a loan with a covenant.
  • This, in turn, would require the entity to determine if the liability is a loan arrangement (see EX 18.144.1) and, if so, to determine if the contingent settlement condition is a covenant (see FAQ 18.144.2).
  • The current or non-current classification of a loan with a covenant is explicitly addressed in paragraph B100 of IFRS 18 (see paras 18.144 to 18.155).
  • If the liability with a contingent settlement condition is not a loan, or the contingent settlement condition is not a covenant, the general classification guidance in paragraph 101(d) of IFRS 18 should be applied.
  • The guidance in paragraph 101 of IFRS 18 is not clear on how an entity should consider a right to defer settlement that depends on the occurrence or nonoccurrence of a contingent event in the 12 months after the end of the reporting period.
  • Accordingly, the current or noncurrent classification of this type of liability would depend on the facts and circumstances of the arrangement.
  • As part of the analysis of the facts and circumstances of the arrangement, it might be important to determine whether the entity has discretion over the contingent settlement condition to defer settlement for at least 12 months after the end of the reporting period: If the entity has discretion over the contingency, it has the right to defer settlement of the liability for at least 12 months after the end of the reporting period.
  • Accordingly, the liability would typically be classified as a non-current liability.
  • If the entity does not have discretion over the contingency (for example, if the counterparty has discretion or neither party has discretion), further judgement might need to be applied to determine the appropriate classification depending on the facts and circumstances.
  • For example, in some cases the entity might classify the entire liability as a current liability (see FAQ 18.134.2), while in others it might classify a portion of the liability as current, based on the entity’s expectation of the rights and obligations in the 12 months after the end of the reporting period (see example 1 in FAQ 18.144.2).

7.6.9 — Does an equity conversion option affect classification of the liability component?

Does an equity conversion option affect classification of the liability component? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.136.1 IFRS 18 · paras 101, para B108 IAS 32 · para 28

  • The instrument has an equity component – being the holder’s right to convert the instrument into a fixed number of the issuer’s equity instruments at any time before the maturity date – and a liability component, being the entity’s obligation to deliver cash to the holder at the maturity date.
  • Conversion features that are at the holder’s discretion do not impact the classification of the liability component of a convertible instrument if that conversion option satisfies the ‘fixed for fixed’ criteria to be classified as equity (see chapter 43 para 38 onwards). [IFRS 18 para B108].
  • If the conversion option satisfies the ‘fixed for fixed’ criteria, the liability component of the convertible debt should be classified as non-current.
  • This is because it is repayable in more than 12 months and the component of the instrument that is classified as equity should be ignored.
  • Any equity components are accounted for in the same way as if they had been issued as separate instruments.
  • The presentation should be the same as if they had been issued as separate instruments.
  • Question 2 If the conversion feature cannot be classified as equity in accordance with IAS 32, does the conversion option affect the classification of the liability?
  • The conversion feature fails the ‘fixed for fixed’ criteria and therefore would not be classified as an equity instrument by the issuer.
  • Although the terms of the liability could result in settlement in the issuer’s own equity instruments, because that alternative is not classified as an equity instrument, the terms of the convertible feature are taken into account in the classification of the liability.
  • The instrument can be converted by the holder at any time in the next 12 months; the liability will therefore be classified as current.

7.6.10 — Should an entity present warranty and environmental provisions as current or non-current in the…

Should an entity present warranty and environmental provisions as current or non-current in the… PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.137.1 IFRS 18 · para B96

  • Presentation of warranty and environmental provisions as current or non-current in the statement of financial position depends on the facts and circumstances as explained below: 1.
  • Warranty provisions – The classification will depend on the warranty’s terms.
  • Warranties that guarantee product performance for a 12-month period are classified as current liabilities.
  • The provision for a warranty that covers a longer period should be split into its current and non-current elements, giving consideration to the length of the normal operating cycle. 2.
  • Provision for environmental liabilities – This type of provision is unlikely to be part of a single operating cycle.
  • It should be split into its current and non-current elements, depending on the likely timing of the expenditure to meet the environmental obligation.

7.6.11 — How do refinance agreements influence presentation of bank debt?

How do refinance agreements influence presentation of bank debt? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.142.1 IFRS 18 · paras 101(d), para B101

  • The loan should be classified as non-current.
  • The entity has the right to roll this obligation over into the new loan facility, even though the loan is due for repayment within six months after the end of the reporting period.
  • The debt does not have to be settled until the new three-year committed facility expires.
  • Question 2 Would the answer be different if the existing loan and new facility were with different banks?
  • Refinancing of the loan in this situation would require its settlement (in substance and in fact) concurrently with a new borrowing.
  • The new borrowing could not be viewed as an extension of the existing loan.
  • The loan should be classified as current in the entity’s statement of financial position, but the circumstances and availability of the new facility should be disclosed.
  • Similarly, if the loan was in the form of commercial paper that typically has a maturity of 90 to 180 days, it would be classified as a current liability.
  • This is because it is likely that the new financing would be provided by a different bank.

7.6.12 — Are all conditions that are related to the repayment of a loan considered to be covenants?

Are all conditions that are related to the repayment of a loan considered to be covenants? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.144.2 IFRS 18 · paras 101(d), paras B99, B100

  • In some cases, judgement might need to be applied (see FAQ 18.134.4).
  • A covenant is typically intended to protect the lender by requiring repayment of the loan earlier than the contractual maturity date where certain conditions are not met by the borrower.
  • These conditions typically relate to the borrower’s financial condition or performance and early repayment is triggered when there has been a deterioration in the conditions.
  • Example 1 An entity has a long-term loan with a bank where the terms of the loan require repayments to be made based on a percentage of sales at the end of each quarter.
  • The loan has no minimum quarterly repayments and is due in full in 5 years’ time.
  • The variable repayments are the mechanism for determining the repayment schedule for the loan rather than as a mechanism to protect the lender.
  • As revenue increases, debt repayments increase.
  • The commercial rationale behind the variable payment mechanism is to allow the borrower to repay the loan in proportion to the improvement of the borrower’s cash flows.
  • Therefore, the liability would not be classified as current or non-current in accordance with the guidance on covenants in paragraph B100 of IFRS 18.
  • Rather, the general guidance in paragraph 101(d) of IFRS 18 would apply.
  • Because this relates to a loan with a contingent settlement condition that is not a covenant, an acceptable treatment would be to classify the estimated repayments to be made in the 12 months after the end of the reporting period as a current liability.
  • While paragraph B104 of IFRS 18 states that a liability is classified as non-current even if management intends or expects to settle the liability within 12 months after the reporting period, this is in relation to voluntary repayments.

7.6.13 — How should loans subject to change of control clauses be classified in the statement of financial…

How should loans subject to change of control clauses be classified in the statement of financial… PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.145.1 IFRS 18 · paras 101(d), para B100

  • Some loan agreements include a change of control clause, under this a loan becomes repayable if there is a change of control event.
  • An entity is unable to prevent a controlling shareholder from selling its shares to a third party, even if there is no expectation that a change of control might happen within 12 months.
  • A change of control clause does not result in classification as a current liability if there has been no change of control event at the end of the reporting period.
  • In this respect, a change of control clause is similar in substance to a loan covenant.
  • IFRS 18 requires classification as a current liability if there is an actual breach at the reporting date, but not if there is only a potential breach.
  • A borrowing is classified as non-current if the counterparty does not have a right, as of the reporting date, to demand repayment within 12 months of that date.

7.6.14 — How should loans with material adverse change and subjective acceleration clauses be classified?

How should loans with material adverse change and subjective acceleration clauses be classified? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.145.2 IFRS 18 · paras 101(d), para B100

  • The entity will need to analyse each clause individually, as at the reporting date, to assess whether the lender has the right to demand repayment of the loan.
  • This assessment would need to consider the application of the MAC clause or SAC, based on conditions that exist at the reporting date.
  • If, based on conditions that exist at the reporting date, an entity concludes that there is no breach of conditions as a result of the application of the MAC clause or SAC at the reporting date, the loan would be classified as non-current.
  • However, if, based on conditions that exist at the reporting date, the entity concludes that the lender has the right to demand immediate repayment under the MAC clause or SAC at the reporting date, the loan should be classified as current.
  • In other words, if the lender has such a right, at the reporting date, the borrower does not have the right to defer settlement of the loan for at least 12 months after the reporting date.
  • Because MAC clauses and SACs are generally based on subjective criteria, giving the lender more discretion in assessing whether a MAC clause or SAC could be invoked, judgement might be required in the assessment.
  • In making this judgement, entities might need to carefully consider the legal rights provided to the lender as a result of the MAC clause or SAC.

7.6.15 — How should a loan balance be classified where the entity has a covenant breach before the end of…

How should a loan balance be classified where the entity has a covenant breach before the end of… PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.145.3 IFRS 18 · para B100(a)

  • An entity has a long-term loan with a bank.
  • The terms of the loan require quarterly testing of certain covenant ratios.
  • The bank requires the entity to file covenant compliance certificates within 60 days of the measurement date of the covenants.
  • The loan should be classified as current.
  • The entity was, in effect, in breach of its covenants at 31 December 20X0, even though reporting of the breach was not required until after the end of the reporting period.
  • This is the case, even though the reported financial figures were not finalised until January 20X1.
  • The entity did not have the right to defer settlement of the loan for at least 12 months after the end of the reporting period; the loan should therefore be classified as current.

7.6.16 — How should a loan balance be classified where the loan agreement includes an additional period to…

How should a loan balance be classified where the loan agreement includes an additional period to… PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.145.4 IFRS 18 · para B100(b)

  • A term loan agreement includes a provision that the borrower must sell a foreign branch of its operations by 31 December 20X0.
  • However, the agreement states that the borrower is permitted an additional two months to complete the sale, if it is not able to sell the branch by that date.
  • The borrower has not been able to find a buyer by 31 December 20X0.
  • The entity should continue to classify the loan balance as non-current.
  • Based on the original contractual provisions, the borrower is only required to complete the sale by 28 February 20X1.
  • The borrower is only required to comply with the condition after the reporting period, therefore, the condition does not affect the entity’s right to defer payment of the liability at the end of the reporting period.
  • The entity should consider the impact of the potential breach and it might need to provide disclosures to allow users of the financial statements to understand the risk that the liabilities could be repayable within 12 months after the reporting period (see para 18.154).

7.6.17 — How should a roll over facility with covenants be classified?

How should a roll over facility with covenants be classified? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.145.5 IFRS 18 · paras 101, paras B100, B101

  • The liability is classified as non-current, taking the following into account: The maturity of the loan is in less than 12 months.
  • However, entity A has the right at its 31 December 20X0 reporting date to extend (or roll over) the facility for a period ending longer than 12 months after the reporting date.
  • Because the entity has a right (in the form of the rollover) to defer settlement for longer than 12 months after the reporting date, the entity needs to consider whether there are covenants related to that right.
  • The right to roll over is conditional on a covenant.
  • However, entity A is only required to comply with the covenant at a date after the reporting date.
  • Therefore, the covenant requirement does not affect the existing right to defer at the reporting date.
  • Entity A therefore has an existing right to defer settlement for at least 12 months after the reporting date.

7.6.18 — How should loans be classified where the lender waived a breach of covenants during the period?

How should loans be classified where the lender waived a breach of covenants during the period? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.148.1 IFRS 18 · para B100

  • The loan should be classified as non-current.
  • The bank has waived the breach of covenant before the end of the reporting period.
  • Based on the terms of the modified agreement, the entity is not in breach.
  • Because of this, the bank does not have an enforceable right to require repayment within the next 12 months at the end of December.
  • A potential new breach of covenant at the end of March does not change the contractual rights of the parties at the end of December.
  • Disclosures of defaults and breaches of loan agreements under IFRS 7 are covered by chapter 47 para 67 onwards.
  • In addition, the entity might need to provide disclosures to allow users of the financial statements to understand the risk that the liabilities could be repayable within 12 months after the reporting period (see para 18.154).

7.6.19 — How should a loan be presented where the lender provided a period of grace before a specific…

How should a loan be presented where the lender provided a period of grace before a specific… PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.148.2 IFRS 18 · paras B100, B103

  • Classification will depend on the length of the period of grace that the lender has provided.
  • Even though a period of grace has been given, the breach of covenant is still reported.
  • The lender can still enforce its rights to demand repayment under the loan agreement at the end of the grace period.
  • A period of grace must be at least 12 months after the reporting date for the loan to be presented as non-current.
  • This is illustrated in two scenarios.
  • Scenario 1 - The bank agreed not to demand payment for a period of 12 months ending October 20X2.
  • The bank has the right to demand repayment in October 20X2 which is less than 12 months after the 31 December 20X1 reporting date.
  • Scenario 2 - The bank agreed not to demand payment for a period of 16 months ending February 20X3.
  • The loan will be non-current.
  • The bank has provided entity A with a period of grace that ends 12 months after the 31 December 20X1 reporting date.
  • Covenants that entity A is required to comply with during 20X2 are not considered in determining the classification at 31 December 20X1.

7.6.20 — Which transactions with owners should be presented in the statement of changes in equity?

Which transactions with owners should be presented in the statement of changes in equity? PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · FAQ 18.163.1 IFRS 18 · para 112

  • An entity presents the following transactions with owners (in their capacity as owners) in the statement of changes in equity: Share issues and redemptions.
  • Purchase and sale of treasury shares.
  • Equity component of convertible bonds issued.
  • Dividends on instruments classified as equity.
  • Credit entries reflecting the issue of equity instruments in connection with an equity-settled share-based payment arrangement.
  • Transactions with non-controlling interests that do not result in a change of control.

7.6.21 — Classification of liabilities as current or non-current: illustrative guide

Classification of liabilities as current or non-current: illustrative guide. PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · EX 18.134.3 IFRS 18 · paras 101, 102, paras B96-B108

  • The classification of a liability as current or non-current is based on an entity’s enforceable rights and obligations at the end of the reporting period, as illustrated by paragraphs 101, 102 and B96-B104 of IFRS 18.
  • Some liabilities are presented as current based on the entity’s business model, for example, liabilities that are part of the entity’s working capital or liabilities that are held primarily for the purpose of trading.
  • Specific requirements apply for rollover facilities or loan arrangements with covenants.

Illustration: 7.6.22 — Determining whether an arrangement is a loan arrangement PwC MOA 2026 Ch18 · EX 18.144.1 IFRS 18 · paras 101(a), paras B100, B101

Facts

  • Background The term ‘loan arrangement’ is not defined by IFRS Accounting Standards.
  • Therefore, in some cases, judgement might need to be applied to determine whether a liability is within the scope of the requirements for loan arrangements.
  • This determination is important for classification of the liability as current or non-current, because it is only loan arrangements with covenants that are within the scope of paragraph B100 of IFRS 18.
  • The need to potentially apply judgement in determining whether or not an arrangement is a loan arrangement is illustrated in the following two scenarios.
  • In both scenarios, it is assumed that the cash received did not result in derecognition of the related receivables under IFRS 9.
  • Scenario 1: Entity A enters into a five-year debtors factoring facility, where it can sell eligible debtors in each period.
  • The credit terms of the debtors are 30 days.
  • Eligible debtors are legally valid debtors that do not exceed 90 days outstanding.

Assessment

  • Collections can be netted against draw-downs for the sale of new debtors in the same period.
  • As a result, the net cash inflow and outflow in each period would be small.
  • Debtors not paid within 90 days are ‘sold’ back to the entity and included in the net settlement of each period.
  • Scenario 2: Entity B enters into a five-year term loan facility, whereby funds can be drawn down in each period, provided that the principal outstanding does not exceed eligible debtors.
  • The entity is only required to repay the shortfall if there is any breach of the covenant (that is, if borrowings exceed eligible debtors).
  • Question Are the arrangements in these two scenarios considered to be loan arrangements for the purposes of classification in IFRS 18?
  • The arrangement in Scenario 1 is legally structured as a factoring arrangement; whereas, in Scenario 2 the arrangement is legally structured as a term loan.
  • Considering only the way the arrangements have been described an entity might conclude that the arrangement in Scenario 2 is a loan arrangement and that arrangement in Scenario 1 is not a loan arrangement.
